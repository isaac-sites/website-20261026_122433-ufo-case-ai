What If the Best UFO Explanation Contradicts the Evidence?

A convincing UFO explanation can fail when reliable timing, position or movement evidence contradicts the proposed cause.

Published 22 Oct 2026

On this page

  • How verified timing, viewing direction and movement contradictions can rule out a proposed UFO identification
  • Why historical UFO similarities and high AI confidence scores cannot outweigh contradictory measurements
  • When unresolved contradictions justify an unexplained verdict rather than a weak or plausible identification

Introduction

A proposed UFO explanation should be rejected when reliable evidence contradicts something essential about the proposed object or event. An aircraft cannot explain a sighting if its verified position, timing and flight path are incompatible with the observation. A satellite cannot account for a light travelling in a demonstrably different direction. A balloon identification becomes doubtful if accurately measured movement cannot be reconciled with the relevant winds.

The strength of that conclusion depends on the quality of the conflicting evidence. A calibrated recording or independently verified position carries more weight than an approximate recollection. Apparent contradictions caused by clock errors, viewing angles or camera effects must also be investigated before dismissing an explanation.

For AI-assisted UFO investigations, the central rule is that a strong resemblance is not enough when dependable measurements disagree. Rejecting the leading explanation may justify leaving the sighting unexplained, but it does not establish an extraordinary origin.

NASA’s [2023 independent UAP study]science.nasa.govOpen source on nasa.gov. supports this evidence-led approach, emphasising the importance of reliable observations, sensor characteristics and independent measurements when investigating unidentified anomalous phenomena (UAP). NASA Science

Which contradictions can rule out a UFO explanation?

A convincing identification must account for the observation as a whole, rather than matching one attractive detail.

For example, an AI system might identify a satellite because a witness described a bright, silent object moving steadily across the night sky. That resemblance provides a useful hypothesis. However, if an accurately timestamped recording shows the object travelling in a direction incompatible with the satellite’s calculated position and trajectory, the identification fails.

The same principle applies to aircraft, drones, balloons, astronomical objects and atmospheric phenomena. The investigator must distinguish between a minor discrepancy that can reasonably be explained and a contradiction that makes the proposed identification physically incompatible with the evidence.

Three kinds of contradiction are particularly important.

Timing: was the proposed object actually there?

Time is often the quickest way to eliminate an otherwise convincing explanation.

Suppose a witness reports a bright object at 21:14, and an automated satellite search finds a visually similar pass at 21:42. If the recording time is independently verified and the satellite’s predicted position is reliable, that satellite cannot explain the recorded event.

However, a phone clock set incorrectly, a recording exported with altered metadata or a witness estimating the time from memory could account for the discrepancy.

Before rejecting the candidate, investigators should establish whether the reported time is:

  • Independently verified through an original recording, synchronised clock or corroborating record.
  • A device timestamp whose time zone and clock accuracy have been checked.
  • An approximate witness recollection with a wider uncertainty range.

A time discrepancy is decisive only when the plausible timing ranges do not overlap.

This is particularly relevant when AI searches satellite catalogues, flight histories or launch records. An exact-looking database match is not proof that the object was visible at the witness’s location and time.

Position: was it in the right part of the sky?

A proposed explanation must also occupy a position consistent with the observation.

If a witness filmed a light above a recognisable building, investigators can use the building’s location, the camera position and the image geometry to estimate the viewing direction. That direction can then be compared with the predicted position of an aircraft, planet or satellite.

Consider an aircraft identified by a flight-tracking system. Its recorded position might place it north-west of the observer while a calibrated video establishes that the unexplained light was south-east.

If the discrepancy exceeds the combined positional and measurement uncertainties, that particular aircraft can be ruled out.

But position checks have important limitations. Camera orientation may be unknown, electronic compasses can be inaccurate, and a witness’s description of a compass direction may be approximate.

A photograph also records angular position rather than distance. An object appearing immediately above a distant landmark may be much closer than the landmark itself.

Consequently, the investigation must test whether the apparent positional contradiction survives realistic changes in camera orientation, observer location and estimated distance.

Movement: could the proposed object behave that way?

Movement contradictions can be more informative than appearance.

An aircraft, balloon and satellite might all appear as a small white point in a recording. Their possible movements, however, differ considerably.

A proposed identification becomes questionable when measured behaviour cannot be reproduced by the candidate under the recorded conditions.

For example, a passive balloon explanation may fail if reliable observations establish sustained movement strongly against the winds at the balloon’s actual altitude, beyond plausible atmospheric uncertainty. A satellite identification may fail if a properly reconstructed trajectory requires movement inconsistent with the satellite’s orbit.

The important qualification is that apparent movement is not necessarily physical movement.

Camera rotation, zoom, tracking systems, perspective and motion parallax can make ordinary objects appear to accelerate, hover or travel at extraordinary speeds.

NASA’s UAP study specifically highlights the need to understand sensor limitations and optical effects before drawing conclusions about unusual behaviour. NASA Science

The correct test is therefore whether the measured movement contradicts the explanation after accounting for the observing equipment and viewing geometry.

What real UFO investigations reveal about conflicting evidence

Two documented investigations illustrate why apparently compelling observations must be tested against reconstructed movement and why disproving unusual behaviour is different from identifying the object itself.

The 2015 Go Fast video: apparent speed versus measured speed

In January 2015, a US Navy F/A-18F recorded an unidentified object over the Atlantic Ocean near Florida using an infrared camera.

The footage appeared to show an object travelling rapidly above the ocean.

In February 2025, the US Department of Defense’s All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO) published a detailed reconstruction.

Its analysis placed the object approximately 13,000 feet above sea level, rather than close to the ocean surface. It estimated wind-compensated movement ranging from approximately 5 to 92 miles per hour, depending on the assumed aircraft heading.

AARO concluded with high confidence that the object did not demonstrate anomalous speed. It attributed the impression of rapid movement to motion parallax, where the movement of the observing aircraft changes the object’s apparent motion against the background. AARO

The significant distinction is between what the video initially seemed to show and what the reconstruction supported.

The interpretation of an object travelling at extraordinary speed was inconsistent with AARO’s calculated geometry and movement. Yet AARO did not claim to have definitively identified the physical object.

Its published methodology also acknowledged limitations, including missing original recording data and uncertainty about the aircraft’s exact position and heading.

The appropriate lesson is not that every unusual video can be explained through parallax. It is that a striking visual interpretation can be rejected without resolving every question about the object.

The 2013 Puerto Rico video: an apparent split and water entry

On 26 April 2013, an infrared sensor aboard a US Customs and Border Protection aircraft recorded objects near Rafael Hernández Airport in Aguadilla, Puerto Rico.

The footage appeared to show a fast-moving object separating into two and entering or emerging from the ocean.

AARO’s March 2025 resolution reached a different assessment after reconstructing the aircraft’s movement, sensor viewing direction and objects’apparent trajectories.

The published findings distinguished three separate questions:

  • Speed: The objects’reconstructed movement was approximately 8 miles per hour, consistent with local wind conditions.
  • Separation: AARO assessed that two nearby objects were visible, rather than one object physically splitting.
  • Water entry: Its reconstruction placed the objects over land throughout the observation, rather than entering the sea.

AARO assessed with high confidence that the objects showed no anomalous behaviour or movement between air and water. It assessed with moderate confidence that they were two sky lanterns. AARO

This difference in confidence is important.

The evidence was considered strong enough to reject the interpretation of extraordinary movement, but less conclusive about the precise identity of the objects.

The report also described factors affecting the infrared image, including changing distance, scattered cloud and reduced thermal contrast. These helped explain why objects could appear to disappear without physically entering the water.

The case demonstrates that investigators should not force every conclusion into a single confidence rating. They may be highly confident that a particular reported behaviour did not occur while remaining less certain about what was actually observed.

Why AI confidence and historical similarities cannot override measurements

AI can make an incorrect UFO explanation look convincing by finding many superficial similarities with earlier cases.

A system trained on historical reports might recognise a familiar combination of features: a bright light, no audible engine, apparently rapid movement and sudden disappearance.

It could then rank an aircraft, satellite or balloon as the most likely explanation.

That ranking may be useful for deciding what to investigate first. It cannot establish that the proposed object was present.

There are three distinct problems.

Historical resemblance is not event-specific proof. Two sightings can have similar descriptions while involving entirely different causes. An earlier report attributed to a balloon does not establish that a new sighting involved a balloon, especially if the new object’s measured direction contradicts the local wind.

AI confidence may measure resemblance rather than physical compatibility. A model’s reported confidence can depend on training examples, assumptions and the way candidate explanations are scored. Unless its probabilities have been properly calibrated, a displayed 95% confidence score should not be interpreted as a 95% probability that the identification is correct.

Correlated evidence can exaggerate apparent support. Several websites may repeat the same witness account, while multiple AI systems may draw from overlapping records. Agreement among those sources is not equivalent to independent confirmation.

NASA’s 2023 report identified machine learning and artificial intelligence as potentially useful for UAP analysis while stressing the need for high-quality, well-characterised data. NASA Science

For an event-specific investigation, AI should therefore operate under an evidence constraint: a candidate explanation cannot be promoted to a confident identification while a reliable, material contradiction remains unresolved.

A practical system can distinguish between a similarity score and a compatibility assessment.

AI assessment What it establishes Appropriate response
High similarity, no material contradiction The candidate deserves serious consideration Investigate further
High similarity, uncertain discrepancy A match is possible but unverified Check measurements and uncertainty
High similarity, verified contradiction The candidate fails an essential test Reject that identification
Low similarity, strong independent confirmation The object may still be correctly identified Give verified evidence greater weight
No candidate fits, evidence remains reliable The observation remains unresolved Preserve an unexplained classification

This approach prevents a visually persuasive match from concealing a physical inconsistency.

It also avoids the opposite mistake: rejecting a well-supported identification simply because a witness’s original description differs from the reconstructed event.

How to decide whether a contradiction is decisive

A contradiction should not automatically eliminate a candidate. The investigator must first establish whether it is genuine, material and supported by sufficiently reliable evidence.

A useful AI-assisted workflow follows five decisions.

  1. Define the exact claim being tested. Record the proposed object, its predicted position, appearance, movement and timing. Avoid vague labels such as’probably an aircraft’when a specific flight or trajectory is available.

  2. Identify the conflicting observation. Specify the relevant video frame, witness statement, sensor measurement or independent record. Record its source, timestamp and uncertainty.

  3. Check whether the measurements are comparable. Convert times to a common standard, distinguish compass bearings from camera-relative directions, and compare ground speed with ground speed rather than confusing it with airspeed.

  4. Test reasonable error ranges. Allow for clock drift, camera orientation, location uncertainty, atmospheric effects and missing data. Determine whether any credible combination of those uncertainties allows the candidate to fit.

  5. Record the decision and its limits. Reject the candidate if an essential contradiction remains. Otherwise retain it as plausible or weakly supported, clearly documenting what prevents confirmation.

The most important step is testing uncertainty rather than comparing two numbers as though both were exact.

For example, a satellite predicted at an azimuth of 240° does not necessarily contradict a witness who remembers looking west. But it may contradict a calibrated recording that establishes a viewing direction of 90° with a narrow error range.

When missing data is not contradictory evidence

Absence of supporting information is often confused with evidence against an explanation.

Suppose a flight-tracking website shows no aircraft near a sighting. That does not establish that no aircraft was present.

The US Federal Aviation Administration explains that Automatic Dependent Surveillance–Broadcast (ADS-B) has equipment and coverage limitations. Some aircraft may not be equipped, and surveillance displays do not necessarily show every aircraft in an area. Federal Aviation Administration

The distinction matters:

  • No aircraft appears in an incomplete tracking record: the aircraft hypothesis remains unconfirmed.
  • A specific aircraft’s verified trajectory is incompatible with the sighting: that aircraft can be rejected.
  • Independent, sufficiently complete surveillance evidence excludes relevant aircraft within the required area and period: the broader aircraft explanation may be substantially weakened.

The same logic applies to incomplete satellite catalogues, missing weather observations and unavailable camera metadata.

A gap in a database is not a verified contradiction.

When should conflicting evidence leave the sighting unexplained?

Rejecting the strongest candidate does not automatically make a sighting unexplained. Investigators must consider whether other explanations remain viable and whether the original observation is sufficiently reliable to support a meaningful assessment.

Three outcomes are particularly relevant.

A specific explanation is ruled out, but alternatives remain

A verified directional contradiction might eliminate a particular satellite while leaving aircraft, drones or other sources possible.

The case should not be labelled unexplained merely because the first candidate failed.

Instead, the rejected identification should be preserved in the case record, with the precise reason for rejection. Remaining candidates can then be assessed independently.

The evidence is too weak to resolve the contradiction

Suppose two witnesses report conflicting movement directions, but neither account has a reliable timestamp or fixed visual reference.

Investigators cannot confidently determine which description is accurate.

In this situation, the contradiction may reveal limitations in the evidence rather than unusual behaviour by the object.

France’s national space agency UFO investigation service, GEIPAN, distinguishes cases that cannot be identified because of insufficient information (category C) from cases remaining unidentified after investigation (categories D1 and D2). Its published methodology also considers the consistency and reliability of the evidence. cnes-geipan.fr

An AI system should preserve that distinction rather than treating every disagreement as evidence of an anomaly.

Reliable evidence excludes the leading explanations

A stronger unexplained verdict becomes appropriate when the observation is sufficiently documented, reasonable conventional explanations have been tested, and material contradictions prevent confident identification.

For example, imagine a sighting with two independently timestamped videos from known locations. Both recordings establish the same object and allow its position and movement to be reconstructed.

If the reconstruction contradicts the trajectories of proposed satellites and aircraft, while balloon and astronomical explanations also fail relevant observational tests, the remaining case may warrant an unexplained classification.

That assessment should still distinguish between explanations that have actually been excluded and possibilities that cannot be tested because relevant records are unavailable.

GEIPAN’s classification guidance allows for reassessment when new evidence becomes available. cnes-geipan.fr

This is an important safeguard. An unexplained verdict is a conclusion about the current evidence, not a permanent claim that no conventional explanation could ever exist.

What evidence could overturn the assessment?

A rejected identification should be reconsidered if new information directly resolves the contradiction that caused its rejection.

The most useful new evidence is usually specific rather than merely additional.

An original video file might establish that a timestamp was incorrect. A surveyed camera location could reveal that an apparent compass-direction conflict arose from faulty orientation. New flight records might identify an aircraft absent from public tracking websites.

Similarly, calibrated measurements from a second viewing position could settle whether apparent acceleration was genuine or caused by perspective.

Reassessment should be symmetrical. New evidence can strengthen an unexplained verdict by confirming that a contradiction is real, or weaken it by showing that the contradiction arose from measurement error.

The case record should therefore retain the original observation, the proposed explanation, the decisive measurements, the estimated uncertainties and the reason for the decision. This makes later AI-assisted reanalysis possible without silently replacing earlier findings.

The central distinction is between rejecting an explanation because the evidence disproves it and withholding an identification because the evidence cannot establish it.

Both are legitimate investigative outcomes. Neither requires an extraordinary cause.

A reliable UFO investigation succeeds when its conclusions remain consistent with the best available measurements, even when that means rejecting an attractive explanation and leaving the sighting unresolved.
