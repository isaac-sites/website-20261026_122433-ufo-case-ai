Why Do Some UFO Sightings Remain Unexplained After Investigation?

GEIPAN's classifications distinguish UFO sightings with too little evidence from those that remain unidentified after detailed investigation.

Introduction

France’s official UFO investigation service, GEIPAN, leaves some sightings unexplained because no tested explanation adequately accounts for the available evidence. Crucially, it distinguishes these cases from reports that cannot be identified simply because essential information is missing.

GEIPAN operates within France’s national space agency, CNES, and investigates reports of unidentified aerospace phenomena, commonly called UFOs or UAPs. Its classification system separates identified sightings, probable explanations, insufficiently documented reports and observations that remain unidentified after investigation.

This distinction matters because an unexplained sighting is not automatically evidence of extraordinary technology. A report may resist identification because witnesses cannot reliably estimate distance, photographs lack context, or available records cannot establish what was in the sky.

GEIPAN’s approach offers a useful model for AI-assisted UFO investigation: measure the strength of the evidence separately from the difficulty of explaining it. GEIPAN

How GEIPAN distinguishes insufficient evidence from unexplained sightings

GEIPAN uses a classification methodology refined in 2008. Rather than judging a sighting solely by how unusual it sounds, investigators assess two characteristics: the consistency of the evidence and the unexplained strangeness remaining after conventional explanations have been considered.

Consistency concerns the quantity and reliability of available information. Investigators consider the precision of witness accounts, supporting photographs, the number of witnesses, whether witnesses are independent and whether different accounts agree.

Residual strangeness describes how poorly the strongest known explanation fits the observation. GEIPAN evaluates candidate explanations involving natural phenomena, human activity and perceptual effects. An observation that initially seems extraordinary may become ordinary once its circumstances are reconstructed.

GEIPAN’s official methodology describes a strangeness measure between zero and one, with values above 0.5 indicating that no proposed explanation is sufficiently convincing. However, high strangeness alone is not enough to establish a category D case: the evidence must also be sufficiently consistent. This is an investigative assessment, not a statistically calibrated probability that an object has an extraordinary origin. GEIPAN

Consider two hypothetical reports. One witness describes a bright object moving rapidly but cannot establish the observation time or viewing direction. Another reports an unusual object whose timing and position are independently corroborated, yet aircraft, satellite and astronomical checks produce no satisfactory match.

Both reports may remain unidentified, but their evidential meaning differs. The first cannot be reliably tested; the second has evidence against which explanations can be evaluated.

GEIPAN’s categories are designed to preserve precisely this distinction.

What separates category C from D1 and D2 cases?

GEIPAN’s categories represent different investigative outcomes rather than a simple scale of how mysterious a sighting appears.

Classification Meaning Evidential significance
A Identified Evidence supports a definite explanation
B Probably identified A conventional explanation is convincing, although definitive proof is missing
C Insufficient information The available evidence cannot support a reliable conclusion
D1 Unidentified after investigation A moderately strange observation with sufficient consistency to retain as unexplained
D2 Unidentified after investigation A highly strange observation supported by stronger, more consistent evidence

GEIPAN’s official English-language FAQ illustrates D1 with cases involving a single witness or no photograph, and D2 with cases supported by several independent witnesses and photographs. These are examples, not rigid requirements: classification depends on the overall strength of the evidence and the remaining difficulty of explanation. geipan.fr

The distinction is particularly important for category C. A case can sound extremely unusual yet still be unsuitable for detailed analysis. An unidentified light recorded without a reliable date, location or viewing direction cannot meaningfully be matched against aircraft movements, satellite passes or astronomical conditions.

Category D requires a different judgement. GEIPAN’s published methodology states that D1 and D2 classifications involve field investigations, witness meetings and cognitive interviews intended to recover and assess observational details. Expert consultation can also contribute to the final decision.

Even this more intensive process cannot guarantee identification. A witness may describe an event accurately while the physical evidence remains too limited to establish its cause. Equally, an apparently consistent account may contain mistaken assumptions about speed, distance or motion.

GEIPAN therefore allows classifications to be revised when new evidence becomes available. A D case is an unresolved investigative outcome, not a permanent declaration that ordinary explanations are impossible. cnes-geipan.fr

The striking comparison is between categories C and D. There are almost ten times as many insufficiently documented cases as cases remaining unexplained after investigation.

These figures should not be interpreted as the probability that a randomly observed UFO is extraordinary. GEIPAN’s database contains cases submitted to and processed by a particular organisation. Its published statistics exclude files still being investigated, and some straightforward reports are resolved without publication.

The proportions also change as investigations are completed and older cases are reconsidered. GEIPAN’s earlier institutional account reported that 50 previously unexplained cases had been reinvestigated and explained in 2017. This demonstrates how accumulated expertise, better records and new analytical methods can change historical classifications. GEIPAN

GEIPAN’s searchable archive makes these distinctions visible at the individual-case level. For example, its records list the 8 August 2012 observation at Volx as D1, while the 8 April 2025 observation at Lugny-Champagne is classified C. The labels indicate different evidential outcomes; they do not establish that the Volx observation involved an extraordinary object or that the Lugny-Champagne witness observed nothing. GEIPAN

Why investigation and AI cannot always produce an answer

GEIPAN’s own explanations identify several practical obstacles: collecting decisive details from witnesses, comparing competing hypotheses, involving suitable specialists and working within limited investigative resources.

Human perception is especially important. A distant light may appear to move unexpectedly because of viewing conditions or perceptual effects. Without a reliable distance estimate, apparent speed cannot establish actual speed. Investigators therefore need to distinguish what a witness directly observed from what the witness inferred. GEIPAN

GEIPAN can consult external specialists and records concerning weather, aviation, astronomical conditions, satellites and space debris. These checks can identify conventional causes that would otherwise remain obscure. Yet the absence of a matching record is not necessarily evidence that no conventional object was present: historical records may be incomplete, and some activities may not be publicly documented. geipan.fr

For AI-assisted investigation, GEIPAN’s categories suggest three particularly useful safeguards.

  • Assess evidence quality before classification. An automated system should flag missing times, uncertain coordinates, absent camera metadata and conflicting accounts before assigning confidence to an explanation.
  • Keep candidate explanations separate from confirmed identifications. A satellite pass or aircraft movement matching the approximate time is a useful lead, but its direction, duration and observed behaviour must also fit.
  • Preserve unresolved cases for reassessment. New flight records, witness evidence or improved image analysis may justify changing a classification, but the earlier uncertainty should remain documented.

GEIPAN’s historical database can also support AI-based similarity searches. A new report describing a stationary luminous object could be compared with earlier cases involving astronomical misidentification or perceptual effects. Such comparisons help investigators decide which hypotheses deserve attention, but resemblance alone cannot establish that two observations had the same cause.

The organisation’s statistics also expose a potential problem in automated classification: a model trained on historical case labels might learn to reproduce past investigative decisions without understanding why the evidence supported them. Category C and category D must therefore remain separate outcomes rather than being merged into a single unexplained class.

NASA’s 2023 independent UAP study similarly emphasised the importance of better-quality data and rigorous scientific analysis. AI can help organise and compare observations, but it cannot reconstruct measurements that were never collected. NASA Science

GEIPAN’s central lesson is that unexplained is an evidence-dependent classification, not an extraordinary-origin verdict. Category C acknowledges that the evidence cannot sustain a conclusion. Categories D1 and D2 preserve observations for which investigation has not established a satisfactory explanation despite a more substantial evidential basis.

That distinction makes GEIPAN’s archive valuable for investigating new UFO reports: it demonstrates not only how conventional explanations can be found, but also when investigators should stop short of claiming an answer they cannot support.
