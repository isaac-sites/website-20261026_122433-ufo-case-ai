When Should a UFO Sighting Stay Unexplained?

AI can compare earlier UFO cases and rank possible explanations, but missing records, weak matches and uncertain evidence can leave a sighting unresolved.

Published 22 Oct 2026

On this page

  • Finding comparable historical UFO reports and testing whether earlier explanations fit the new sighting
  • Ranking candidate explanations by timing, location, observed behaviour, independent corroboration and conflicting evidence
  • Distinguishing identified, plausible, weak and unresolved outcomes without treating missing data as proof of something extraordinary

Introduction

A UFO sighting should remain unexplained when the available evidence cannot support a reliable identification, even after reasonable investigation of known possibilities. That does not mean the object was extraordinary, extraterrestrial or operating beyond known physics. It means investigators cannot establish what caused the observation with sufficient confidence.

The crucial distinction is between a sighting that cannot be explained because information is missing and one that remains unusual despite substantial evidence and careful testing.

Artificial intelligence can help distinguish these outcomes by comparing historical UFO reports, ranking possible explanations and detecting contradictions. However, a convincing AI-generated match is not necessarily a correct identification.

NASA’s 2023 study of unidentified anomalous phenomena (UAP) emphasised that limited high-quality observations make firm scientific conclusions difficult. The appropriate response to insufficient evidence is to preserve uncertainty, not manufacture an answer. NASA Science

When should investigators leave a UFO sighting unexplained?

The decision should reflect both the quality of the evidence and how closely any proposed explanation matches the observation.

A useful classification system separates cases into five outcomes:

Outcome What the evidence establishes Appropriate decision
Identified Reliable, independent information supports a specific object or phenomenon Close with a documented explanation
Plausibly explained A known cause matches the main observations, but confirmation is incomplete Record the leading explanation and its uncertainty
Weak match An explanation shares some characteristics but has significant gaps or contradictions Keep the hypothesis tentative
Unresolved — insufficient data Missing or unreliable information prevents a meaningful comparison Record the evidence limitations without claiming an anomaly
Unresolved — investigated Adequate evidence supports analysis, but no tested explanation accounts for the observations satisfactorily Retain as unexplained and eligible for reassessment

This distinction closely resembles the approach used by GEIPAN, the UFO investigation service associated with France’s national space agency, CNES.

GEIPAN identifies cases under categories A and B when an explanation is established or considered probable. Category C covers observations that cannot be identified because information is insufficient. Category D covers observations left unidentified after investigation. Its more detailed classification also distinguishes D1 and D2 according to the strength and unusual nature of the remaining evidence. GEIPAN

The distinction matters because an absence of information is not the same as positive evidence of unusual behaviour.

A brief video of an unidentified light might remain in the insufficient-data category because there is no reliable viewing direction, distance or recording time. By contrast, an observation supported by calibrated instruments and independent witnesses may justify more extensive investigation if the strongest conventional explanations conflict with measured facts.

Neither outcome establishes an extraordinary origin.

How many sightings remain unexplained?

GEIPAN’s published figures, updated to 31 March 2026, classify approximately 66.5% of its cases as explained or probably explained, 30.3% as unidentified because of insufficient information, and 3.2% as unexplained after investigation. These percentages describe GEIPAN’s case records, not all UFO sightings worldwide. GEIPAN

The contrast is revealing. Cases lacking enough information substantially outnumber those retained as unexplained following investigation.

For AI-assisted investigations, this suggests that the first decision should not be whether an observation looks extraordinary. It should be whether the available evidence is strong enough to support a meaningful conclusion.

Can earlier UFO cases explain a new sighting?

Historical UFO reports provide valuable comparisons because apparently unfamiliar observations often resemble previously investigated events.

AI can search archived descriptions for similar appearances, movement patterns, durations and observing conditions. The purpose is not merely to find an earlier report that sounds alike. It is to establish whether the explanation reached in the earlier case could reasonably account for the new observation.

Comparing behaviour rather than appearance alone

Consider a hypothetical report of three bright lights travelling silently across the sky before disappearing in sequence.

An automated search might retrieve earlier accounts involving satellites, aircraft formations, drones, balloons and atmospheric effects.

Several matches could look convincing. Their investigative value depends on more specific comparisons.

  • Satellite explanation: Does the observation time and viewing direction correspond to an actual orbital pass? Could the reported disappearance result from the satellites entering Earth’s shadow?
  • Aircraft explanation: Were aircraft operating in the relevant airspace, and would their lighting and flight paths match the reported pattern?
  • Drone explanation: Is the movement compatible with small unmanned aircraft, and is there independent evidence of activity at the location?
  • Balloon explanation: Could recorded winds and the likely viewing distance account for the movement and apparent formation?
  • Perception explanation: Could apparent changes in speed or direction arise from viewing geometry, limited visual references or camera movement?

Historical similarity helps identify hypotheses worth testing. It cannot establish that the same cause occurred twice.

GEIPAN’s methodology explicitly assesses possible known physical and perceptual causes while considering how well each hypothesis accounts for the reported observation. GEIPAN

The historical comparison trap

An archived case may resemble a new sighting closely while remaining unsuitable as evidence for its explanation.

For example, a 1990s report of a bright triangular formation might be associated with aircraft. A newer report describing a similar formation could involve drones, satellites or another cause entirely.

The common description does not make the underlying events equivalent.

Historical databases also contain uneven reporting standards. Some records preserve precise dates, directions and original evidence, while others contain only brief witness summaries. Investigators should therefore distinguish between:

  • Cases with independently verified explanations.
  • Cases with credible but unconfirmed explanations.
  • Cases that were never conclusively assessed.
  • Repeated or copied versions of the same original account.

An AI similarity system that treats these records equally could give unsupported explanations excessive weight.

A particularly important safeguard is to prevent circular reasoning. If an earlier report was labelled a satellite sighting merely because it resembled other presumed satellite reports, that label should not become independent confirmation when the new case is assessed.

When historical comparisons change the verdict

A useful historical comparison contributes more than a familiar description.

Suppose an earlier sighting was explained through a combination of satellite-position calculations, witness viewing direction and photographic timing.

That earlier investigation provides a testable mechanism. The new sighting can be checked against equivalent orbital and observational conditions.

If those conditions agree, the satellite explanation becomes stronger.

If the predicted satellite positions conflict with the witness’s recorded direction, the historical resemblance should carry much less weight.

This is where AI-assisted comparison is particularly useful: it can retrieve the earlier explanation, identify the conditions required for it to work, and highlight which of those conditions are satisfied or contradicted by the new case.

The system should therefore rank explanations by their supporting evidence, not simply rank archived reports by textual similarity.

How should AI rank competing explanations?

A useful ranking considers how well each candidate explanation fits the actual event, how much independent evidence supports it and what important contradictions remain.

The strongest hypothesis is not automatically correct merely because the alternatives score lower.

Five dimensions are especially important.

Comparison factor Evidence that strengthens a hypothesis Evidence that weakens it
Timing Recorded activity overlaps the sighting within known time uncertainty Candidate activity occurs outside the credible observation window
Location and direction Predicted position agrees with the witness’s viewpoint Position or travel direction conflicts with reliable measurements
Observed behaviour Speed, path, brightness changes and duration are compatible Verified behaviour cannot be reproduced by the proposed cause
Independent corroboration Separate records or instruments support the same event Identification rests on a single unverified account or repeated copies
Conflicting evidence Apparent inconsistencies have testable explanations Significant verified contradictions remain unresolved

These factors should not necessarily receive equal weight.

A verified positional contradiction may be more important than several broad similarities in colour or shape. Likewise, radar observations with usable calibration information may carry more weight than an estimate of speed based solely on watching a distant light.

NASA’s independent study emphasised the need for reliable observations and contextual information. The US All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO) has similarly identified limitations in sensor information as a major obstacle to resolving reported sightings. NASA Science

Why confidence scores can be misleading

Imagine an AI system evaluating a fictional sighting and producing these illustrative compatibility scores:

Candidate explanation Illustrative score Main limitation
Aircraft 75/100 No matching flight independently confirmed
Drone 65/100 Operator and position unknown
Satellite 40/100 Reported movement conflicts with expected track
Balloon 30/100 Recorded wind direction appears inconsistent

The aircraft hypothesis ranks highest, but the score does not establish a 75% probability that an aircraft caused the sighting.

Unless the scoring system has been properly calibrated against representative cases with independently established outcomes, its numerical results are only relative indicators.

An especially dangerous mistake is forcing all available explanations to add up to 100%. That assumes the correct explanation must already be among the candidates.

In practice, the relevant object may not have appeared in accessible aviation records, the sighting time may be wrong, or an important candidate may not have been considered.

A responsible system therefore needs an explicit option for none of the proposed explanations is sufficiently supported.

It should also record whether high confidence concerns a narrow finding or the object’s actual identity. Investigators might be confident that an object did not travel at extraordinary speed while remaining unable to identify it.

What should count as contradictory evidence?

Contradictions become meaningful when they involve reasonably reliable observations rather than unsupported assumptions.

For example, a witness describing an object travelling at enormous speed has not necessarily established its actual speed. Without a reliable distance estimate, apparent angular movement cannot determine physical velocity.

However, two calibrated cameras observing the same object from different positions may allow investigators to estimate distance and movement more reliably.

Likewise, a missing aircraft entry is not proof that no aircraft was present. Public flight-tracking records may be incomplete, and an absence of a record is only strong negative evidence when the relevant source had sufficient coverage to detect the proposed object.

The proper question is whether the available information genuinely conflicts with a hypothesis, not merely whether supporting information has failed to appear.

What real investigations show about uncertainty

Actual UAP case assessments demonstrate why it is important to separate identification, unusual-looking imagery and evidence quality.

The’Go Fast’recording: unusual appearance versus measured behaviour

The widely discussed’Go Fast’infrared recording, captured by a US Navy aircraft, appeared to show an object moving rapidly across the scene.

AARO’s subsequent analysis examined viewing geometry and parallax, the apparent displacement of an object caused by a change in the observer’s position.

AARO concluded with high confidence that the object did not exhibit exceptional or anomalous behaviour. However, the agency could not calculate a single exact trajectory from the available information, and the object’s identity was not conclusively established. AARO

This case illustrates an important distinction: investigators can successfully challenge an extraordinary interpretation without identifying the object itself.

The sensible outcome is not to declare that every question has been resolved. It is to document which claims the evidence supports and which questions remain open.

Middle East infrared recordings: data may not establish whether an object was present

AARO’s published case material includes recordings from the Middle East in 2023 and 2024 that remain unresolved.

The 2024 case involves approximately six minutes and forty-two seconds of infrared footage showing an apparent thermal contrast. According to AARO, the available information cannot establish whether the signature originated from a physical source or an artefact of the sensor.

The 2023 recording presents a comparable difficulty. AARO reports that neither recording has sufficient corroborating telemetry or information from different sensor types to support a conclusive assessment. AARO

These cases are particularly relevant to automated image analysis.

AI may detect a shape, track a bright region or describe apparent movement accurately without establishing that the image feature represents a separate physical object.

A video can contain measurable visual information while still lacking the evidence needed for identification.

This is why AI image recognition should not convert every detected feature into a definite claim about a flying object’s size, speed, material or origin.

The difference between difficult and impossible to analyse

Some cases become solvable when additional records are obtained. Others may remain permanently uncertain because essential information was never captured.

AARO’s assessment of the Al Taqaddum imagery from Iraq illustrates how supplementary footage, environmental information and detailed analysis can change the interpretation of an initially unfamiliar observation. The agency ultimately assessed the recorded object’s appearance and behaviour as consistent with balloons. AARO

By contrast, when a recording lacks necessary sensor information and no independent observations exist, repeated analysis of the same limited footage may produce increasingly elaborate theories without adding meaningful evidence.

More computation cannot necessarily recover information that was never recorded.

What evidence justifies keeping a case open?

An investigation should remain open, or be classified as unexplained after investigation, when important observations cannot be satisfactorily reconciled with the leading explanations and the available evidence is sufficient to make that disagreement meaningful.

The decision becomes stronger when several conditions are met.

The original observation can be reconstructed. Investigators know enough about the time, location, viewing geometry and sequence of events to test specific hypotheses.

Important evidence is independently supported. Separate witnesses, original recordings, instruments or contemporaneous records provide information that does not simply repeat one source.

Common explanations have been tested properly. Aircraft, satellites, balloons, drones, astronomical objects, atmospheric effects and relevant perceptual explanations have been assessed using information appropriate to the particular event.

Remaining contradictions are substantive. The unresolved features involve reasonably well-established observations rather than uncertain estimates of distance, size or speed.

The investigation is documented. Another investigator can understand why an explanation was accepted, rejected or left undecided.

GEIPAN’s approach connects the remaining unusual nature of a report with the consistency and quality of its evidence. In particular, it requires stronger supporting information when a case is considered more difficult to reconcile with known explanations. GEIPAN

These conditions do not provide a universal mathematical threshold. They establish an evidence-based standard for deciding whether the unexplained label is justified.

When lack of records should not be treated as an anomaly

A short witness account reporting a silent, rapidly moving object may be impossible to identify if the observation time is approximate and the distance cannot be determined.

AI might search historical cases, satellite catalogues, aviation records and weather information without finding a convincing match.

That failure does not necessarily raise the probability of an extraordinary explanation.

The problem may instead be that the search inputs were too uncertain to distinguish among ordinary possibilities.

For such a case, an appropriate finding would be:

Unidentified because the available information is insufficient to distinguish reliably between several possible explanations.

This is more informative than describing the observation as an unexplained anomaly because it identifies precisely what prevents a conclusion.

When an investigated case deserves further attention

Consider a different hypothetical event involving two independently positioned cameras, accurate timestamps and a measurable trajectory.

Suppose the reconstructed observations are inconsistent with the available aircraft and satellite data, the weather conditions do not support the leading atmospheric explanations, and camera artefacts have been carefully investigated.

This would justify a stronger unresolved classification, provided the observations and relevant data coverage were reliable.

The conclusion would still be limited: no tested explanation adequately accounts for the documented event.

It would not establish that the object violated physical laws or belonged to an unknown technological civilisation.

The value of the unexplained classification is that it preserves a well-defined research problem rather than substituting speculation for missing knowledge.

When should an unexplained case be reopened?

Unexplained should normally be a revisable classification, not a permanent verdict.

New information can change an assessment in either direction. Additional evidence may identify a conventional cause, or it may reveal that a previously accepted explanation was based on incorrect assumptions.

GEIPAN explicitly allows cases in its insufficient-data and unexplained categories to be reassessed when new information becomes available. GEIPAN

For an AI-assisted case file, especially useful reasons to reopen an investigation include:

  • A previously unavailable original video, photograph or instrument recording is recovered.
  • More accurate timestamps, camera positions or witness locations become available.
  • New aviation, satellite or environmental records allow an earlier hypothesis to be checked.
  • A comparable historical case receives a well-supported explanation that can be tested against the current event.
  • An independent witness or source provides genuinely new corroboration.
  • A technical reassessment identifies a sensor artefact, calculation error or mistaken assumption in the earlier investigation.

These changes should trigger a review, not automatic reclassification.

For example, identifying a newly documented satellite near the reported sighting location would strengthen a satellite hypothesis only if its calculated position and behaviour matched the observation.

Equally, discovering that a previously assumed aircraft was elsewhere does not automatically establish that the object was extraordinary. It removes one candidate explanation and requires the remaining evidence to be reconsidered.

A transparent record should preserve the earlier conclusion, the new information and the reason the judgement changed.

The risk of automatically closing cases

Automation can make UFO investigation faster, but it can also introduce premature certainty.

A model trained largely on explained cases may repeatedly favour familiar categories even when a new observation contains significant contradictions. A model trained on sensational or poorly verified case summaries may do the opposite, exaggerating the unusual features of ordinary observations.

The appropriate safeguard is to separate candidate generation from final classification.

AI should identify promising matches, retrieve supporting information, expose conflicts and describe unresolved questions. Investigators should then assess whether the underlying data justify the conclusion.

This is particularly important where an automated result might be presented publicly as an authoritative identification.

A transparent finding of insufficient evidence is preferable to a confident but unsupported explanation.

What should the final case verdict say?

A useful final verdict should answer three separate questions: what was observed with reasonable confidence, which explanations have been tested, and what remains uncertain.

It should make clear whether the case is unresolved because the evidence is inadequate or because a sufficiently supported observation has resisted the explanations examined.

The final record should distinguish:

  • Established facts: Verified timing, location, recordings, instrument measurements and independently supported observations.
  • Candidate explanations: Known objects or phenomena that could account for some or all of the evidence.
  • Reasons for rejecting or weakening candidates: Specific positional, temporal, behavioural or evidential contradictions.
  • Unresolved uncertainties: Missing records, measurement limitations, contested observations and untested possibilities.
  • Conditions for reassessment: The additional evidence most likely to change the decision.

A case may also require a mixed verdict. An investigation might establish that an object’s apparently exceptional speed resulted from parallax while leaving its precise identity unknown. Recording both findings is more accurate than forcing the entire case into a single all-or-nothing label.

The strongest principle for AI-assisted UFO investigation is therefore straightforward: a sighting should remain unexplained for as long as the evidence cannot justify a more specific conclusion, but its unexplained status must never be treated as evidence of an extraordinary origin in itself.

That distinction protects both sides of a responsible investigation. It prevents ordinary observations from being exaggerated, while ensuring genuinely unresolved cases are not dismissed merely because a convenient explanation can be generated.

An unexplained UFO case is ultimately a statement about the present limits of identification. Its scientific value comes from defining those limits clearly enough that new evidence, better comparisons and future investigation can meaningfully test them.
