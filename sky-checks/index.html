Was That UFO an Aircraft, Satellite or Something Else?

AI can compare a UFO sighting with flight paths, satellite passes, astronomical positions and historical weather to test ordinary explanations.

Published 22 Oct 2026

On this page

  • Checking aircraft routes, airport activity, drones and aviation lights against the witness's reported movement
  • Matching satellites, planets, meteors and space activity to the sighting's time and position in the sky
  • Testing clouds, atmospheric optics, balloons and local conditions while distinguishing genuine matches from coincidences

Introduction

Yes. Aircraft, satellites, astronomical objects and weather phenomena can explain many UFO sightings, and checking them against the exact time and location is one of the most effective ways to investigate an unusual light or object in the sky. The crucial question is not whether an ordinary object was nearby, but whether its position, movement, appearance and timing genuinely match the witness’s account.

AI-assisted UFO investigation makes these comparisons faster. Software can reconstruct historical flight paths, calculate satellite positions, identify visible planets and retrieve weather records. It can then flag potential explanations and highlight where the evidence agrees or conflicts.

The results are not always conclusive. Some aircraft are absent from public tracking services, satellite predictions have uncertainties, and weather observations may not capture conditions at the precise viewing location. A credible investigation therefore distinguishes a convincing identification from a possible explanation and an unresolved sighting.

NASA’s [2023 independent UAP study]NASA Science UAP June 16, 2022. https://science.nasa.gov/uap/. Source panel: Citations. Accessed October 20, 2026Open source on nasa.gov. emphasised the importance of better observations, contextual data and scientific analysis. NASA Science

Which explanation should investigators check first?

The quickest starting point is to compare the reported behaviour with the characteristics of familiar objects. Different explanations leave different clues, and those clues determine which records an investigator should examine first.

Possible explanation Typical appearance or behaviour Most useful comparison
Aircraft Flashing lights, steady movement, apparent hovering when approaching head-on Historical flight tracks, bearings, altitude and airport activity
Drones Low-altitude lights, hovering, turns and variable speed Local operations, eyewitness evidence and available drone identification records
Satellites Steady lights moving across the sky, occasional bright flashes or groups of lights Orbital positions, viewing geometry and sunlight conditions
Planets and bright stars Bright, apparently stationary or slowly shifting points, sometimes shimmering Astronomical position at the exact date and time
Meteors and re-entries Brief streaks, fragmentation, glowing trails or several bright objects Fireball reports, trajectory estimates and re-entry records
Balloons Slow drifting, occasional hovering or unexpected changes in apparent direction Wind at relevant altitudes and known launches
Clouds and atmospheric optics Unusual shapes, luminous patches, rings, distortion or apparent movement Cloud observations, satellite imagery, temperature profiles and light-source positions

These are investigative starting points, not identification rules. For instance, a steady light might be a satellite, but it could also be a distant aircraft whose flashing lights are not visible. A glowing object that appears stationary could be a planet, a balloon or an aircraft approaching almost directly towards the observer.

NASA’s [guide to identifying UFOs and UAPs]NASA Science Identifying UFOs and UAPs December 1, 2013. https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/skywatching/night-sky-network/identifying-ufos-and-uaps/. Source panel: Citations. Accessed October 20, 2026Open source on nasa.gov. describes several of these ordinary sources of confusion, including Venus, aircraft, rockets, satellites, meteors and unusual clouds. NASA Science

The most important distinction is between a behavioural resemblance and a verified match. A candidate becomes persuasive when its predicted position and motion agree with independent observations.

Could an aircraft explain the reported movement?

Aircraft are a particularly important explanation because their lighting, distance and viewing angle can make ordinary flight behaviour look unfamiliar.

A distant aircraft approaching almost directly towards a witness may seem to hover for several minutes. Its apparent position changes very little even though it is travelling rapidly. As it turns, the visible lights and apparent speed may change suddenly.

Landing lights can look like a single brilliant white object. Navigation lights and anti-collision beacons can produce changing colours or flashing patterns, while atmospheric haze and cloud can obscure the aircraft itself.

How historical flight tracking helps

Modern commercial aviation provides a useful investigative record. Aircraft equipped with Automatic Dependent Surveillance–Broadcast (ADS-B) transmit position-related information that can be collected by air traffic systems and independent receiver networks.

For a UFO report, an AI-assisted system can search historical flight records within the sighting’s time window, retrieve candidate trajectories and calculate how each aircraft should have appeared from the witness’s position.

The comparison should consider:

  • Time: Was the aircraft present during the reported observation, including reasonable uncertainty in the witness’s clock?
  • Bearing: Did the aircraft appear in the correct compass direction?
  • Elevation: Was its height above the horizon compatible with the sighting?
  • Motion: Did its apparent path, speed and changes of direction agree with the account?
  • Lighting: Were the observed colours, flashes and brightness plausible for its orientation and distance?
  • Duration: Could the aircraft remain visible for the reported length of time?

A flight passing several kilometres from the witness is not automatically the explanation. What matters is the line of sight. A more distant aircraft directly ahead of the observer may be a stronger candidate than a nearer aircraft travelling across another part of the sky.

The US Federal Aviation Administration’s [ADS-B technical guidance]Federal Aviation Administration ADS-B FAQ. https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/technology/adsb/faq. Source panel: Citations. Accessed October 20, 2026Open source on faa.gov. explains the surveillance system and its coverage and equipment limitations. Federal Aviation Administration

A documented example: aircraft mistaken for distant objects

The US All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO), which investigates unidentified anomalous phenomena for the US Government, has published a case involving objects recorded over the western United States.

Its analysis combined video footage with commercial flight records. AARO concluded that the three distant objects corresponded to separate commercial aircraft, with their radar tracks aligning with the recorded objects.

This illustrates why aircraft identification should not depend on visual appearance alone. Objects that are only small points in a sensor recording can become identifiable when the sensor’s direction and the relevant flight trajectories are reconstructed together. AARO

Why a blank flight map does not rule out aircraft

Public flight-tracking websites are useful, but they are not complete historical records of everything airborne.

An aircraft may be missing because its equipment does not broadcast the expected data, a receiver network lacks coverage, or the public service has filtered or omitted relevant information. Military, police, emergency and privately operated aircraft may present additional difficulties, depending on their equipment and reporting arrangements.

A more thorough investigation may therefore check airport movements, relevant airspace notices, published aviation activity, local aviation organisations and corroborating witness reports.

No aircraft visible on a public flight map means only that no suitable aircraft was found in that dataset. It does not establish that the airspace was empty.

What about drones?

Drones can produce behaviours that ordinary fixed-wing aircraft do not: prolonged hovering, abrupt changes of direction, short movements between stationary positions and flights close to buildings or trees.

Drone identification presents a different challenge. A small drone is unlikely to appear in a conventional commercial aircraft tracking archive.

Some jurisdictions use electronic drone identification systems. For example, the FAA’s Remote ID framework requires many registered drones in the United States to broadcast identification and location information, but receiving or preserving those broadcasts requires suitable equipment and coverage. FAA

For UK investigations, relevant checks may include local drone activity, the operator or venue involved, airspace restrictions and applicable Civil Aviation Authority requirements.

A drone explanation becomes stronger when an independently documented operation matches the sighting. A general statement that drones can hover is insufficient to identify a particular object.

Could a satellite or planet have been mistaken for a UFO?

Satellites and astronomical objects can be checked with unusually precise geometry. Their expected positions can be calculated from the date, time and observer’s location, making them valuable candidates in an AI-assisted investigation.

However, identifying something in the correct part of the sky is only the first step. Visibility, brightness, motion and duration must also fit.

Satellites: predictable paths, sometimes surprising lights

Many visible satellites appear as small points travelling smoothly across the night sky. They are generally visible because they reflect sunlight while the observer is in darkness.

This is why evening and early-morning sightings deserve particular attention. A satellite can remain illuminated above the Earth even after sunset at ground level.

Satellite constellations, particularly SpaceX’s Starlink network, can create additional confusion. Recently deployed satellites may appear in a line or group, while reflective surfaces on individual satellites can produce bright flashes.

AARO’s fiscal-year 2025 report, published in 2026, describes the growing importance of satellite reflections in UAP investigations. It reports that improved three-dimensional modelling enabled analysts to resolve 238 previously reported cases as satellite flaring. Those findings concern AARO’s investigated material, not a representative sample of every UFO sighting worldwide. Residual Cases

Satellite flaring is especially relevant to reports of bright objects appearing and disappearing. A satellite does not need to accelerate, stop or switch on a light to create this effect. The reflection can become visible only when the angles between the Sun, spacecraft and observer align.

How an automated satellite match works

An investigation can use orbital data from sources such as [CelesTrak]celestrak.orgOpen source on celestrak.org. to calculate the positions of catalogued objects.

A useful comparison reconstructs the satellite’s apparent path from the witness’s coordinates, checks whether it was sunlit and estimates when it should have entered or left the visible portion of the sky.

For example, suppose a witness describes a white light moving from west to east for roughly four minutes before suddenly disappearing. A predicted satellite pass becomes a strong candidate if it follows the same path, overlaps the reported time and disappears near the observed position as it enters Earth’s shadow.

The disappearance is particularly valuable evidence because it provides an additional test beyond the direction of travel.

Satellite pass services such as [Heavens-Above]heavens-above.comOpen source on heavens-above.com. express predictions in terms of time, brightness, azimuth and elevation. Azimuth means compass direction; elevation is the angle above the horizon. Visible Passes for Specific Satellite

There is an important historical limitation. Orbital datasets, often called two-line element sets or TLEs, are periodically updated. Using today’s orbital information to reconstruct an event months or years ago can introduce significant errors, particularly if the satellite manoeuvred or its orbit changed.

Research published through CelesTrak has demonstrated that orbital predictions carry errors that depend on the quality and age of the underlying data. Investigators should prefer orbital information close to the sighting date and preserve the uncertainty in the reconstruction. CelesTrak

Planets: bright objects that appear to hover

Venus is a frequent source of confusion because it can shine extremely brightly, often near the horizon around sunrise or sunset. Jupiter and bright stars such as Sirius can also attract reports.

Atmospheric turbulence may make low astronomical objects appear to flicker or change colour. Movement of the witness, nearby trees and the lack of a stable visual reference can make a distant point of light appear to shift unexpectedly.

NASA’s identification guidance discusses Venus, Sirius and Jupiter as familiar objects that can be mistaken for UFOs. NASA Science

An astronomical comparison should reconstruct the object’s predicted position rather than rely on its prominence in the sky. If the witness reports a bright light in the south-west at 20:30, software can determine whether Venus, Jupiter or a bright star was present at that bearing and elevation.

A planet is a convincing explanation for a persistent light that barely changes position, but not for a reliably documented object crossing a large portion of the sky in seconds.

Meteors, fireballs and re-entries

Brief luminous events require a different comparison. Meteors typically last seconds, while spacecraft re-entries can produce longer-lasting luminous trails and multiple fragments.

The [American Meteor Society]amsmeteors.orgOpen source on amsmeteors.org. notes that meteor reports are sometimes confused with sunlit aircraft condensation trails. Duration provides a useful distinction: a fireball generally lasts only seconds, whereas a condensation trail may remain visible for minutes or longer. American Meteor Society

AI can compare the sighting with fireball reports from other observers, recorded re-entries and launch information. A cluster of independent reports at similar times but from different locations can help reconstruct a meteor’s path.

Rocket launches provide another possibility. Exhaust plumes illuminated by the Sun at high altitude can appear unusually large or luminous, even when observers are already in darkness.

The strongest evidence is an independently recorded launch or re-entry whose timing, geography and apparent direction agree with the report. A launch somewhere in the world on the same evening is not enough.

Can weather make an ordinary object look extraordinary?

Weather can explain UFO reports in two distinct ways. First, the atmosphere can create unusual visible phenomena, such as luminous rings, bright patches and remarkably shaped clouds. Second, weather can distort or obscure aircraft, planets, satellites and other ordinary objects, making their appearance or movement harder to interpret.

Historical weather checks are therefore not simply about establishing whether the sky was clear or cloudy. They help determine what could have been visible, how light might have behaved and whether an object could have moved as the witness described.

Clouds, reflections and atmospheric optics

Some atmospheric effects produce shapes and lighting patterns that look unfamiliar even to experienced observers.

The Met Office identifies several relevant phenomena involving the reflection, refraction, scattering and diffraction of light. Refraction means that light changes direction as it passes through materials with different optical properties; diffraction describes how light spreads around small obstacles or through openings. Met Office

Particularly relevant examples include:

  • Lenticular clouds: Smooth, lens-shaped clouds that often form in airflow over hills or mountains. They can remain nearly stationary even when the surrounding wind is strong.
  • Sun dogs: Bright patches that form on either side of the Sun when sunlight passes through ice crystals. They can resemble additional luminous objects.
  • Haloes and coronas: Rings or coloured structures around the Sun or Moon caused by interactions with ice crystals or tiny water droplets.
  • Illuminated cloud layers: Clouds that reflect light from the Moon, aircraft, ground-based lighting or the low Sun, sometimes hiding the source itself.
  • Mirages and atmospheric distortion: Unusual temperature structures that bend light and alter the apparent position or shape of distant objects.

A witness reporting an unusually smooth, disc-shaped object above a mountain may have seen a lenticular cloud. Yet finding favourable weather conditions alone would not justify that conclusion. The investigator needs a compatible cloud formation, viewing direction and observation time.

Similarly, a bright patch beside the setting Sun could be a sun dog, but only if its angular position and the atmospheric conditions support that explanation.

What historical weather records can establish

An AI-assisted system can retrieve observations from nearby weather stations, archived satellite and meteorological datasets.

For UK sightings, the [Met Office historical data resources]metoffice.gov.ukOpen source on metoffice.gov.uk. include station records and the MIDAS-Open archive. International investigations can use NOAA’s [Global Historical Climatology Network hourly dataset]www.ncei.noaa.gov Global Historical Climatology Network hourly (GHCNh). https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/products/global-historical-climatology-network-hourlyNational Centers for Environmental Information (NCEI) August 4, 2…, which includes observations of cloud, visibility, wind and other conditions from monitoring stations. Met Office

The key weather variables depend on the proposed explanation.

Reported observation Weather information that matters Investigative value
A light suddenly vanishes Cloud layers, visibility, precipitation Tests whether cloud obscuration is plausible
A floating object moves slowly Wind direction and speed at several heights Tests balloon drift
A bright object shimmers or changes colour Atmospheric stability, haze and viewing elevation Tests optical distortion
A disc-shaped object appears stationary Cloud imagery, wind flow and terrain Tests a lenticular cloud explanation
A glowing patch appears near the Sun or Moon Cloud type, ice-crystal conditions and light-source position Tests haloes, sun dogs and coronas

Weather-station observations have limits. A report from an airport ten miles away may not represent conditions over a particular village, valley or coastline. Hourly measurements can also miss short-lived changes.

Consequently, the strongest assessment combines surface observations with cloud imagery, radar where relevant and local witness evidence.

Wind is essential when assessing balloons

Balloons are an important alternative to aircraft and drones because they can drift, rotate, reflect sunlight and change their apparent movement as they rise or descend.

The key mistake is to compare balloon movement only with wind measured at ground level. Wind direction and speed can change substantially with height.

Suppose a witness reports a bright object moving east, pausing and then moving north. A balloon may be plausible if archived upper-air observations show different wind directions at the altitudes it could have reached.

AI can test several possible balloon heights and reconstruct corresponding drift paths. This does not prove that a balloon was present, but it can determine whether the proposed movement is physically reasonable.

An especially persuasive identification would include a known balloon launch, a predicted trajectory and a close match to the witness’s description.

AARO’s 2025 annual report records balloons among its frequently identified objects, ranging from small reflective party balloons to larger research balloons. Residual Cases

Temperature inversions and unusual apparent movement

A temperature inversion occurs when air temperature increases with altitude over a particular layer rather than decreasing in the usual way.

The Met Office explains that inversions can form under high-pressure conditions and trap cooler air near the surface. These atmospheric structures can also contribute to conditions in which light or radio signals propagate unusually. Met Office

For a sighting involving a distant light near the horizon, atmospheric refraction may be relevant to its apparent position, shape or visibility.

Atmospheric effects also matter when investigating instrument evidence. The US National Weather Service describes anomalous radar propagation, in which unusual temperature or moisture gradients bend radar beams, potentially producing misleading returns from the ground. Glossary

This does not mean that every unexplained visual observation or radar contact is a mirage. It means atmospheric conditions should be tested before treating unusual apparent positions or sensor readings as evidence of exceptional movement.

How can AI distinguish a real match from a coincidence?

The greatest value of automation comes from testing several explanations against the same observation, rather than searching until one vaguely similar event appears.

A responsible investigation begins with the witness’s original account and converts it into a time-and-position comparison.

Consider a hypothetical sighting in southern England. At approximately 21:15, an observer reports a bright white object travelling slowly towards the east for four minutes before disappearing.

Several explanations might initially fit: an aircraft, a satellite, a distant planet obscured by cloud or a drifting balloon.

An AI-assisted workflow can test them in a consistent sequence.

  1. Reconstruct the observation. Record the witness’s coordinates, local time, time uncertainty, initial and final bearings, duration and any video timestamps. Convert the time to Coordinated Universal Time while preserving the original local-time information.
  2. Search aviation activity. Retrieve historical tracks and compare predicted aircraft positions with the reported path. Check whether an approaching or turning aircraft could explain the apparent speed.
  3. Reconstruct the night sky. Calculate planetary positions and satellite passes. Check whether the satellite would have been illuminated and whether its predicted disappearance agrees with the account.
  4. Test weather and balloon movement. Examine archived cloud cover and visibility, then model balloon drift using wind at plausible heights.
  5. Compare the competing explanations. Record agreement and disagreement for timing, bearing, elevation, movement, appearance and duration.
  6. Identify the decisive missing evidence. Determine whether a verified flight track, original recording, independent witness or better historical dataset could separate the remaining candidates.

The system might find an aircraft passing through the relevant compass direction at 21:12 and a satellite crossing nearby at 21:16.

It should not automatically choose the closest timestamp. If the aircraft’s apparent position would have remained near the horizon while the witness describes an object high overhead, the aircraft is a weak match. If the satellite crosses the correct portion of the sky and becomes obscured by Earth’s shadow at the reported disappearance point, that explanation is considerably stronger.

A time match is useful; a matching trajectory and disappearance mechanism provide much stronger evidence.

Why viewing geometry matters more than apparent speed

One of the most important lessons from documented UAP investigations is that apparent speed can be misleading.

When the observer or camera is moving, a distant object can appear to travel rapidly across the background. This is known as parallax: an apparent shift in position caused by a change in viewpoint.

AARO’s analysis of the US Navy’s 2015 Go Fast video provides a useful example. Although the footage prompted questions about the object’s apparently rapid movement over the ocean, the agency’s subsequent geometric analysis concluded with high confidence that the object showed no exceptional or anomalous behaviour.

The report acknowledged that the available data were insufficient to determine every aspect of the object’s position and motion. Its conclusion was about the absence of demonstrated exceptional performance, not a definitive identification of the object itself. AARO

This distinction matters when AI analyses phone footage or aircraft-mounted camera recordings. Pixel movement alone cannot establish an object’s physical speed.

Without reliable distance, camera orientation and observer movement, estimates of extreme acceleration may be unjustified.

When should a sighting remain unresolved?

An investigation should not force an ordinary explanation simply because one is available.

A plausible match has to survive meaningful attempts to disprove it. If no candidate adequately explains the observed time, direction, duration and behaviour, the case should remain unresolved until better evidence is available.

A practical classification system can distinguish the following outcomes.

Assessment Meaning Example
Confirmed identification Independent records closely match the observation across its important characteristics A flight track and original video show the same trajectory at the same time
Strongly supported explanation Most decisive characteristics agree, with limited remaining uncertainty A predicted satellite pass closely matches the route and disappearance
Plausible explanation The candidate is physically possible but lacks decisive confirmation Wind conditions permit balloon drift, but no balloon is documented
Weak or coincidental match Only broad similarities exist, or important details conflict An aircraft was nearby but followed the wrong apparent direction
Ruled out Reliable evidence contradicts the candidate A planet was below the horizon throughout the event
Unresolved Available evidence does not support a sufficiently convincing identification Video shows movement that cannot be reconstructed reliably because distance and orientation are unknown

These categories prevent a common error: treating the failure to identify an ordinary cause as proof of an extraordinary one.

An unresolved case may simply lack adequate observations. It may also contain genuine behaviour that current records cannot account for. Those are different circumstances and should not be combined.

AARO’s fiscal-year 2025 report illustrates the importance of this distinction. Of 319 reports received during its reporting period, 114 were resolved, nine were referred for further specialist analysis and 191 were placed in an active archive because available information was insufficient for a confident assessment. Residual Cases

An archive entry does not mean that an object demonstrated extraordinary technology. Equally, closing other cases as ordinary objects does not automatically explain those that remain open.

The strongest reasons to question an ordinary explanation

Certain observations deserve closer investigation after aircraft, satellite and weather screening.

An independently measured trajectory that conflicts with all relevant candidate paths is more significant than a witness’s general impression of unusual speed.

Multiple recordings from different positions can also be valuable because they may establish distance and three-dimensional movement. A single recording frequently cannot distinguish a nearby small object from a distant large one.

Other potentially decisive evidence includes a reliable multi-sensor observation, a clearly documented duration inconsistent with a proposed meteor, or a satellite candidate that was below the horizon during the entire sighting.

But apparent acceleration, unusual brightness or an absence from flight-tracking websites are not sufficient on their own. Each observation must be evaluated alongside the limitations of the witness’s perspective and recording equipment.

What makes a sky-check result trustworthy?

The reliability of an AI investigation depends on both the quality of the underlying records and the way the comparisons are performed.

There are three particularly important safeguards.

Preserve uncertainty in time and location. A reported sighting at 22:00 might mean exactly 22:00 or simply sometime around that hour. An automated system should search an appropriate interval and show whether a candidate remains compatible across it. The same applies to uncertain viewing directions.

Distinguish observed data from modelled results. A recorded aircraft position, a calculated satellite trajectory and a reconstructed balloon drift are not equivalent forms of evidence. The first is a surveillance observation, subject to data limitations; the second is an orbital prediction; the third may depend on assumed balloon height and wind conditions.

Retain contradictory information. If a potential aircraft matches the timing but conflicts with the object’s reported colour, path or elevation, the case record should display that disagreement rather than hide it behind a single confidence score.

A useful AI explanation might therefore read:

A satellite pass is strongly supported by the reported time, direction and duration. Its predicted path agrees with the witness’s account, including the approximate disappearance point. The identification remains provisional because the sighting time is uncertain by two minutes and no original video is available.

This is more informative than an unsupported probability such as’95% satellite’. Numerical confidence scores become meaningful only when investigators can explain how they were calculated and whether they have been tested against reliably identified cases.

Historical case comparison can add useful context. If an earlier sighting involved a similar sequence of lights and was conclusively identified as a satellite formation, AI can suggest that pattern for consideration. The earlier case does not prove that the new sighting has the same cause.

NASA’s UAP study calls for improved data quality and scientific methods, reinforcing the principle that better measurements are more valuable than more confident speculation. NASA Science

What the sky checks can actually establish

Aircraft tracking, satellite calculations, astronomy and historical weather records can explain a substantial range of unusual sightings, particularly reports involving lights, distant objects and apparent motion.

Each source answers a different question. Aviation records can identify aircraft travelling through a line of sight. Orbital calculations can test whether a satellite was visible and illuminated. Astronomical calculations can locate planets and bright stars. Weather data can establish conditions compatible with cloud phenomena, atmospheric distortion or balloon movement.

Their limitations are equally important. Aircraft tracking does not record every flight. Not every drone or balloon has a public movement history. Archived orbital predictions carry uncertainty, and weather observations may not describe the precise patch of sky a witness saw.

The strongest finding is therefore a specific, reproducible match: an identified object or natural process whose expected position, timing, movement and appearance are consistent with the sighting.

Where these checks produce contradictory or incomplete results, the appropriate outcome is a documented unresolved case, not a forced identification.

That is the central advantage of AI-assisted sky checks. They can rapidly eliminate weak explanations, identify strong ordinary candidates and preserve the genuinely uncertain observations for closer human investigation.
