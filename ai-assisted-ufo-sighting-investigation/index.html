Can AI Help Explain a Mysterious UFO Sighting?

AI-assisted UFO sighting investigation uses artificial intelligence, automated data searches and evidence comparison to determine what might explain a specific sighting.

Published 22 Oct 2026

Updated 2026-10-22

Introduction

AI can organise witness accounts, reconstruct timelines, identify possible matches in historical records and highlight contradictions between different sources. It can also help investigators recognise when an apparently unusual object is consistent with a satellite, aircraft, balloon or atmospheric phenomenon.

The important distinction is between identifying a credible explanation and simply finding something that happened nearby. A coincidence is not proof, while an unsuccessful search does not establish that a sighting was extraordinary.

NASA’s 2023 independent study of unidentified anomalous phenomena (UAP) emphasised the need for better observations, reliable contextual information and scientific methods. These principles provide a foundation for using AI responsibly in UFO investigations. NASA Science

How does an AI-assisted UFO investigation work?

A useful investigation begins with one specific event, not a general question about whether UFOs exist. Its purpose is to establish what was observed, reconstruct the circumstances and assess possible explanations using evidence that can be independently checked.

The workflow combines human investigation with automated analysis. AI handles repetitive searching, information extraction and comparison, while investigators judge witness reliability, evidence quality and the physical plausibility of competing explanations.

A practical investigation normally follows six stages:

  1. Record the sighting: Collect the original witness account, location, date, time, duration, direction of observation and any photographs or videos.
  2. Standardise the information: Convert the report into a structured case file, identifying uncertain or missing details.
  3. Reconstruct the event: Establish a timeline, viewing direction, possible movement and relevant geographical features.
  4. Search for known explanations: Compare the observation with weather, astronomical objects, aircraft, drones, balloons, satellites and other recorded activity.
  5. Compare historical cases: Find earlier sightings with similar characteristics and assess whether their explanations apply.
  6. Evaluate the outcome: Rank plausible explanations, identify contradictions and document what remains unresolved.

This broadly follows the investigative principles used by GEIPAN, the French national space agency’s specialist UAP investigation service. GEIPAN uses a reproducible process involving witness testimony, case-file creation, initial analysis, detailed investigation and classification. GEIPAN

The advantage of automation is not that it replaces these stages. It makes them more consistent and allows investigators to examine contextual information that might otherwise be overlooked.

Turning a witness report into a structured case file

The quality of an AI investigation depends heavily on the information supplied at the beginning. A report stating that someone saw a bright object moving strangely provides too little detail for reliable identification.

A structured case file should preserve the witness’s original words while organising the factual elements into searchable fields.

Case-file field Information to record
Date and time Calendar date, local time, time zone and estimated accuracy
Location Observation coordinates, address or approximate area
Witness position Whether stationary, walking, driving or travelling in an aircraft
Duration How long the event lasted, including uncertainty
Viewing direction Compass bearing, elevation above the horizon and changes over time
Appearance Colour, shape, brightness, apparent size and number of objects
Behaviour Movement, hovering, flashing, direction changes or disappearance
Environmental conditions Cloud cover, visibility, wind and nearby lighting
Supporting evidence Original photographs, videos, audio, radar or instrument records
Witness information Number of observers, independent accounts and relevant viewing conditions

Separating observations from interpretations

A particularly important task for AI is distinguishing what a witness actually saw from what they concluded.

For example, consider the statement:

“A silent, triangular spacecraft hovered over the houses before accelerating away.”

The directly reported observations might instead be:

  • Three lights appeared in a triangular arrangement.
  • No sound was heard.
  • The lights seemed stationary for approximately 20 seconds.
  • Their apparent position then changed rapidly.

The original interpretation should remain in the record, but it should not become an established fact.

There may have been three separate lights rather than one triangular object. Silence does not establish the absence of an engine, and apparent acceleration does not necessarily indicate rapid movement through physical space.

AI language models can help separate these elements, but investigators must review the results to ensure that the system has not altered or invented details.

Preserving original evidence

Photographs and videos should be retained in their original form, with the original filenames, timestamps and available metadata.

AI-enhanced versions may help reveal faint features, but enhancement can introduce artificial details. A sharpened edge or reconstructed texture should never be treated as new physical evidence.

Where possible, investigators should also record camera settings, focal length, zoom, frame rate, digital stabilisation and whether an image has been cropped or compressed.

NASA’s UAP study highlighted the importance of sensor calibration and metadata when investigating unusual observations. Without information about how an image was captured, an apparent anomaly may be difficult to distinguish from an imaging artefact. NASA Science

Which environmental checks matter most?

Once the initial case file is complete, automation can search historical data covering the relevant time and place.

The strongest checks are those capable of directly testing a reported object’s position, movement, appearance or timing. Other environmental information may be useful but should receive less weight unless there is a clear mechanism linking it to the observation.

Aircraft, drones and other aviation activity

Aircraft should be among the first possibilities examined when a witness reports moving lights, hovering objects or unexplained formations.

Flight-tracking services such as ADS-B Exchange can provide recorded positions for many aircraft. Its historical data products include archived aircraft-location information that can be compared with a sighting’s time and geographical coordinates. ADS-B Exchange

An automated investigation can compare:

  • Recorded aircraft positions with the witness’s viewing direction.
  • Flight paths with the object’s apparent movement.
  • Aircraft altitude and distance with reported brightness or sound.
  • Airport approaches, departures and holding patterns with repeated observations.
  • Known aviation lighting patterns with the colours and flashing behaviour described.

However, flight-tracking records are not complete inventories of everything airborne. Coverage varies, and some aircraft or drones may not appear in accessible records.

Consequently, finding a matching aircraft can provide strong supporting evidence, but finding no aircraft should not automatically exclude aviation.

Drone activity requires separate attention because smaller drones often lack publicly accessible tracking histories. Public displays, authorised operations and local incident reports may provide useful supporting information.

Satellites, planets and meteors

Astronomical checks are especially valuable when objects appear as bright points, travel steadily without audible sound or seem to remain stationary.

Satellite-identification software can calculate whether a known object was predicted to pass through the relevant part of the sky. Services such as CelesTrak provide orbital information used for satellite tracking and trajectory prediction.

The investigation should consider the satellite’s predicted direction, elevation, visibility and timing. Historical orbital data is preferable to simply reversing today’s satellite positions, because satellite orbits change and prediction accuracy is limited. CelesTrak

Astronomical analysis should also check bright planets, stars, the Moon, meteor activity, satellite re-entries and documented space launches.

An important limitation is that astronomical position alone does not prove identification. A predicted satellite crossing must correspond closely enough with the reported direction, timing and apparent behaviour.

Weather and atmospheric effects

Historical weather data can explain sightings involving clouds, reflections, apparent motion, balloons and unusual lighting.

In the UK, the Met Office maintains weather observations and archives, including daily weather summaries dating back to 1860. Its observational services also provide access to historical meteorological measurements. Met Office

An AI system can examine cloud cover, visibility, wind speed and direction, precipitation, fog and atmospheric conditions at the reported time.

Wind information is particularly useful when an unidentified object appears to drift steadily. If its motion is consistent with wind at a plausible altitude, a balloon becomes a stronger candidate.

Atmospheric conditions may also account for halos, reflections, distorted light sources or unusual views of distant objects.

Nevertheless, weather data needs careful interpretation. Wind measured at ground level may differ greatly from wind higher in the atmosphere, while observations at a distant weather station may not represent conditions at the sighting location.

Local activity, infrastructure and seismic records

Some sightings require additional checks involving activity close to the observation site.

These may include emergency-service incidents, scheduled light shows, industrial operations, electrical faults, power outages, network disruption and temporary lighting installations.

Geographical analysis can identify wind turbines, telecommunications masts, roads, airports, reflective buildings and other features that could affect the view.

Seismic information can also be checked where a witness reports ground shaking, unusual sounds or an alleged relationship with geological activity. The United States Geological Survey provides an earthquake catalogue that supports searches by date, time, location, magnitude and depth. Earthquake Catalog

However, seismic correlation should generally be a secondary investigation rather than a routine leading explanation for an aerial sighting.

An earthquake occurring somewhere in the same region is not evidence that it produced an observed light. Establishing such a relationship would require a credible physical mechanism, appropriate timing and independent observations.

The same caution applies to power failures and network anomalies: proximity in time is a reason to investigate, not proof of a connection.

How AI reconstructs the sighting timeline

One of AI’s most useful contributions is combining information recorded in different formats and at different levels of precision.

A witness may remember seeing an object at approximately 21:30, while their phone video contains a timestamp of 21:27:42. Nearby CCTV may show a flash at 21:28, and an aircraft-tracking database might record a flight crossing the relevant area shortly afterwards.

Automation can place these records on a shared timeline and identify where they agree or conflict.

The process should:

  • Convert local times to a common reference, normally Coordinated Universal Time (UTC).
  • Account for British Summer Time, inaccurate device clocks and estimated witness times.
  • Represent uncertain times as intervals rather than exact moments.
  • Compare the duration of recordings with the claimed duration of the sighting.
  • Distinguish when an event occurred from when it was reported or uploaded.

For UK observations, the distinction between GMT and British Summer Time is particularly important. A one-hour error could produce a completely misleading aircraft or satellite match.

Reconstructing position and apparent movement

Geospatial analysis adds another layer by placing the observer, surrounding landmarks and candidate objects on a map.

A bright light may appear to move rapidly when viewed from a travelling vehicle. Equally, camera rotation and changes in viewing angle can make a distant object appear to accelerate.

This is one reason investigators should distinguish angular movement, which is motion across the observer’s field of view, from an object’s actual speed through space.

A single photograph normally cannot establish an object’s distance or physical dimensions. Without reliable distance measurements, calculations of exceptional speed or acceleration may be unjustified.

Where two or more independently located observers record the same event, their viewing directions may allow triangulation. Such measurements can be substantially more informative than one observer’s estimate of apparent size.

How automation can identify ordinary explanations

Automated explanation screening is most effective when it tests specific, competing hypotheses.

Rather than asking AI to name a mysterious object, the investigator can ask whether the evidence is consistent with an aircraft, balloon, satellite, astronomical object or another documented source.

Each proposed match should be evaluated against the entire event rather than one convenient similarity.

Consider a fictional sighting in Yorkshire at 22:15. A witness describes an orange light moving slowly westward for three minutes before disappearing.

An automated search might identify an aircraft passing through the area, a satellite crossing the sky, and wind conditions that could support balloon movement.

The next stage is not to declare all three equally plausible.

Investigators would compare the aircraft’s recorded bearing and lighting, the satellite’s expected elevation and visibility, and whether the balloon hypothesis explains the reported appearance, movement and disappearance.

A candidate explanation becomes stronger when it accounts for multiple independent observations without requiring unsupported assumptions.

What actual UAP investigations demonstrate

The United States All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO) has published case-resolution material illustrating why contextual analysis matters.

In the 2015 Go Fast incident, a US Navy aircraft recorded an object appearing to travel rapidly over the sea. AARO subsequently assessed that the apparent high speed could be explained through viewing geometry and parallax —the apparent movement of an object caused by changes in the observer’s position.

AARO concluded with high confidence that the object did not demonstrate exceptional or anomalous behaviour, although the available data did not establish every aspect of its location and movement. AARO

Another example is the 26 April 2013 Puerto Rico infrared recording, which appeared to show an object moving rapidly, splitting into two and entering the water.

AARO’s reconstruction concluded that the recording was consistent with two objects moving close together, rather than one dividing into two. It also assessed that the objects travelled approximately with the wind and did not enter the sea. The assessment does not, by itself, establish their precise identity. AARO

These cases demonstrate the distinction between what a recording appears to show and what can be established through geometry, sensor interpretation and external information.

They also show why AI-assisted screening should produce testable explanations rather than simply label an image.

Can AI compare a sighting with earlier UFO reports?

Historical case comparison is another useful application of artificial intelligence.

Traditional searches often depend on exact keywords. A witness describing a “floating orange sphere” might not find an older report mentioning a “slow amber orb”, even though both observations share relevant features.

AI-based similarity search can identify semantic connections between descriptions without requiring identical wording.

An effective comparison system can examine several aspects of a sighting simultaneously:

Comparison feature Investigative value
Appearance Identifies similar descriptions of colours, shapes and lighting
Duration Distinguishes brief flashes from sustained observations
Motion Compares hovering, steady movement and apparent acceleration
Viewing conditions Connects sightings occurring under similar atmospheric or lighting conditions
Geography Identifies recurring reports around particular locations
Time of year Tests whether observations coincide with seasonal activities or astronomical events
Earlier explanations Reveals whether similar reports were identified as aircraft, balloons or other sources

GEIPAN’s published investigation and classification system offers a valuable model because it distinguishes identified cases, probable identifications, cases lacking sufficient information and cases remaining unidentified after investigation. GEIPAN

Historical comparisons are especially helpful when they uncover an explanation that was established independently.

For example, a modern report of regularly spaced lights crossing the sky may resemble earlier observations associated with satellite formations. That comparison can prompt a specific satellite search rather than establish an identification on its own.

Why similar sightings are not necessarily related

A key risk is treating similarity as evidence of a common physical cause.

Many unrelated objects appear as points of light at night. Aircraft, satellites, drones and distant illuminated structures may generate overlapping descriptions.

AI models can also reproduce assumptions contained in their training data. If a historical database repeatedly describes unexplained lights as spacecraft, a language model may inherit that terminology without supporting evidence.

For this reason, comparison results should preserve the original description, any documented investigation, its outcome and the quality of supporting evidence.

Repeated reports are not necessarily independent confirmations. A single widely circulated video can generate multiple secondary accounts that appear to describe separate events.

Good clustering systems must therefore identify duplicate reports, shared media and witnesses who may have influenced one another.

How should AI rank explanations and uncertainty?

Not every possible explanation deserves the same confidence.

An AI system may produce a numerical score, but a percentage is meaningful only if the scoring method has been properly defined and tested.

A score of 90% cannot legitimately mean a 90% probability that an object was an aircraft simply because a language model generated that number.

A more transparent approach evaluates each hypothesis against explicit criteria: temporal agreement, directional agreement, physical plausibility, independent confirmation, contradictions and data completeness.

A practical classification framework is:

Assessment What it means
Identified Strong evidence establishes a particular object or phenomenon
Plausible A known explanation fits the main observations, but confirmation is incomplete
Weak A candidate explains some details but has important gaps or contradictions
Ruled out Reliable evidence conflicts with a necessary part of the explanation
Unresolved Available information cannot establish a satisfactory explanation
Anomalous Reliable evidence indicates characteristics that remain difficult to explain after substantial investigation

These categories are a proposed investigative framework, not a universal official classification.

The difference between unresolved and anomalous is particularly important.

A distant light recorded on a short, blurry video may remain unidentified simply because the available evidence is inadequate. It would be misleading to present that result as strong evidence of unusual physics.

Conversely, a well-documented observation supported by independent instruments may justify more detailed investigation if ordinary explanations have been tested and substantial inconsistencies remain.

GEIPAN’s classification methodology makes a related distinction by evaluating both the consistency of the information and the unexplained characteristics remaining after investigation. GEIPAN

When does a sighting genuinely remain unusual?

A report becomes more scientifically interesting when the remaining uncertainty cannot readily be attributed to missing basic information.

Potentially stronger cases may involve independent observers, synchronised recordings, calibrated instruments, reliable location measurements or consistent observations from different types of sensors.

Even then, apparent extraordinary movement must be established rather than assumed. Measurements need to demonstrate that the object moved unusually, rather than that camera motion, distance uncertainty or sensor behaviour created that impression.

An anomalous classification means that a significant discrepancy remains under the available evidence. It does not confirm extraterrestrial technology, an unknown form of propulsion or any other extraordinary origin.

What can go wrong with automated UFO analysis?

AI can accelerate investigative work, but it can also make incorrect conclusions appear systematic and convincing.

Several weaknesses deserve particular attention.

False matches. A commercial aircraft may have crossed a broad search area during the reported time, but that does not establish that it was visible in the witness’s particular direction. Automated systems need narrow spatial and temporal tests.

Missing historical records. An absence of flight, satellite or weather data may reflect incomplete coverage rather than the absence of a conventional source.

Inaccurate witness estimates. Estimates of distance, altitude and speed are often unreliable without reference points. AI should preserve uncertainty rather than convert impressions into precise measurements.

Misleading image enhancement. Generative enhancement can create features not present in the original photograph or video. Original media must remain the evidential reference.

Selection bias. A system trained heavily on dramatic, unresolved sightings may identify unusual similarities while overlooking ordinary explanations.

Data-source dependence. Two databases may reproduce the same underlying observation. Agreement between them should not be counted as independent confirmation unless their sources are genuinely separate.

Unjustified confidence. A convincing narrative assembled by AI is not equivalent to a verified reconstruction.

These concerns reinforce NASA’s emphasis on accurate sensor information, well-characterised observations and scientifically reliable datasets. NASA Science

A properly designed system should also make it possible to inspect how a conclusion was reached. Investigators need access to the relevant source records, time conversions, search parameters and evidence supporting or contradicting each explanation.

Without this transparency, automation risks creating a sophisticated-looking answer that cannot be independently reproduced.

A practical example: investigating an unexplained light over the UK

Consider a hypothetical report from a witness standing near the edge of a town in southern England.

The witness reports seeing a bright white object at approximately 20:40 on an October evening. It moves silently from north-west to south-east, remains visible for around four minutes and appears to disappear suddenly.

The witness records part of the event using a mobile phone.

An AI-assisted investigation would begin by establishing the exact date, observer location, phone timestamp and approximate viewing direction.

It would then reconstruct the camera’s field of view using visible buildings, trees or other landmarks.

The first automated checks could reveal several possibilities.

Aviation records might show aircraft operating nearby, but none crossing the relevant viewing direction. A satellite-position calculation might identify a candidate whose predicted passage corresponds closely to the reported time and movement.

Weather observations might indicate clear skies, making a high-altitude satellite visible. Astronomical calculations could then help test whether the satellite’s disappearance was consistent with its passage into Earth’s shadow.

These results would support a satellite explanation, but the investigator would still need to compare the predicted brightness, angular path and disappearance time against the recording.

If they agreed closely, the case might reasonably be classified as an identification or strong probable match.

If the timing or direction differed substantially, the satellite explanation would weaken, even if it initially appeared attractive.

The case might then require additional searches for aircraft not represented in public tracking records, drones, balloons or other local activity.

Most importantly, the final report would distinguish confirmed environmental information from candidate explanations and unresolved details.

The process is successful whether it establishes an ordinary cause or produces a well-documented unresolved case.

What a credible final case assessment should contain

A completed AI-assisted UFO investigation should allow another person to understand the reported event, assess the evidence and reproduce the main checks.

The final case assessment should contain the original witness description, a structured event timeline, relevant environmental findings and a comparison of the leading explanations.

Each important finding should identify its source and distinguish between direct observation, independent record, calculated inference and speculative possibility.

A useful final assessment should also explain which explanations were rejected and why. Simply listing an aircraft, satellite or balloon as a possibility provides little value unless the report evaluates how closely it fits the observed event.

The most important unresolved questions should be stated explicitly, particularly where missing media, uncertain timing or incomplete coverage prevents a firmer conclusion.

Privacy also matters. Witness names, exact residential locations and identifying details in should not be published unnecessarily. GEIPAN’s investigative process includes anonymisation before public release, providing a useful precedent for responsible case publication. GEIPAN

The value of AI-assisted UFO investigation

AI-assisted UFO investigation is most useful as a method of evidence organisation, contextual verification and explanation testing.

Its greatest practical benefit is the ability to connect a specific observation with information that is often scattered across separate sources. A witness account, a satellite track, a weather record and an aircraft position can be compared within the same timeline and geographical framework.

This can resolve straightforward cases more efficiently, reduce repetitive manual research and direct attention towards the observations that deserve closer scrutiny.

But artificial intelligence does not eliminate uncertainty. It cannot recover missing measurements, prove that unrecorded aircraft were absent or turn an ambiguous video into a reliable measurement of extraordinary movement.

A credible investigation is therefore judged by the quality of its reasoning, not by whether it produces an identification. Sometimes the evidence supports a familiar explanation. Sometimes several plausible explanations remain. Occasionally, sufficiently reliable observations resist the available conventional explanations.

In each situation, the essential requirement is the same: preserve the original evidence, test competing hypotheses fairly, distinguish established facts from inference and describe the remaining uncertainty without exaggeration.
