Was That UFO Actually an Aircraft?

Historical flight records can reveal whether an aircraft matched a UFO's reported position, movement and timing, but missing tracks leave uncertainty.

Published 22 Oct 2026

On this page

  • Why aircraft can appear to hover, accelerate or change colour
  • Matching historical flight tracks to a witness's viewing direction, elevation and timing
  • What missing flight records and the AARO commercial aircraft case reveal about identification limits

Introduction

Yes, historical flight tracks can identify some UFO sightings as ordinary aircraft. By reconstructing where aircraft were flying at the time of a sighting, investigators can compare their positions, directions, altitudes and movements with what a witness reported. A strong match may explain an apparently hovering light, an unexpected change of direction or several mysterious objects moving together.

The important qualification is that flight-tracking records are incomplete. An aircraft may be absent from a public database because of limited receiver coverage, tracking equipment, privacy restrictions or gaps in archived data. Finding no aircraft on a tracking website does not prove that the sky was empty.

For AI-assisted UFO investigations, historical flight records are therefore a valuable source of independent evidence, not a definitive test on their own. The strongest identifications combine flight data with accurate witness timing, viewing direction, video evidence and the geometry of the observation.

The US government’s All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO) has demonstrated this approach in a documented case involving distant commercial aircraft mistaken for unidentified objects. Its findings illustrate both the usefulness of flight-track reconstruction and the importance of estimating an object’s distance correctly. AARO

Why can aircraft look like UFOs?

An aircraft does not need to perform an unusual manoeuvre to appear mysterious. Its apparent movement depends on where the observer is standing, which direction the aircraft is travelling and how far away it is.

A passenger aircraft travelling hundreds of miles per hour can look almost stationary when it is approaching the observer directly. Conversely, an aircraft crossing the observer’s field of view may appear to move quickly even when travelling at a similar speed.

This difference between actual movement and apparent movement is central to investigating reported UFO behaviour.

Why an approaching aircraft can appear to hover

Imagine standing outside at night and watching a bright white light above the horizon. For several minutes, it seems to remain in almost exactly the same position. Then it begins moving sideways and becomes noticeably dimmer.

One possible explanation is an aircraft approaching along a route that points nearly towards the observer.

During the initial approach, most of the aircraft’s movement is towards the witness rather than across their field of view. Its angular position changes slowly, creating the impression that it is hovering.

When the aircraft turns, its sideways movement becomes more obvious. The observer may interpret this as a sudden acceleration or a stationary object unexpectedly beginning to move.

Distance also matters. An aircraft far away produces a smaller angular movement than a nearby aircraft travelling at the same speed across the observer’s line of sight.

Without reliable distance information, a witness cannot determine the aircraft’s true speed simply by watching its movement across the sky.

Why aircraft lights can change colour or disappear

Aircraft carry several lighting systems that may produce confusing appearances at night.

The US Federal Aviation Administration’s [Airplane Flying Handbook]faa.govOpen source on faa.gov. describes the conventional arrangement of red lights on the left wing, green lights on the right wing and white lights towards the rear. Aircraft may also display flashing anti-collision lights and powerful landing lights. FAA

The combination visible to an observer changes with the aircraft’s orientation.

A witness might initially see a brilliant white landing light, followed by a red or green navigation light as the aircraft turns. At a considerable distance, the aircraft’s body may be invisible, leaving only a point of light.

Atmospheric haze, thin cloud and camera exposure can further alter the apparent brightness or colour.

These effects can account for reports involving lights that:

  • Remain almost motionless before moving sideways.
  • Change from white to red or green.
  • Appear to brighten, dim or vanish.
  • Seem to accelerate without an obvious change in physical speed.
  • Look like separate lights that later merge or become difficult to distinguish.

None of these observations establishes that an aircraft was responsible. They indicate which ordinary mechanisms should be tested against historical flight records.

The key question is whether a particular aircraft’s recorded movements would have produced the reported appearance from the witness’s position.

How can historical flight tracks be matched to a UFO sighting?

Historical flight tracking works by reconstructing aircraft movements during a specific period and comparing those movements with the witness’s view of the sky.

The process is more demanding than finding a nearby aircraft on a map.

An aircraft can be geographically close to a witness but appear in a completely different direction. Another aircraft, much farther away, may align almost perfectly with the reported UFO.

A reliable comparison therefore requires three-dimensional viewing geometry.

Start with the witness’s exact time and position

The investigation begins with a structured sighting record.

At minimum, the case should establish the date, observation time, location and approximate direction of the object. Ideally, it should also contain the object’s apparent height above the horizon, duration, movement, colour and any photographic or video evidence.

The distinction between local time and Coordinated Universal Time (UTC) is particularly important.

Historical aviation datasets commonly use UTC. A sighting reported at 21:30 British Summer Time corresponds to 20:30 UTC. Searching the wrong hour can produce an entirely different set of aircraft.

The investigator should also record how the time was obtained. A timestamp embedded in an original video may be more useful than a time reconstructed from memory, although camera clocks can themselves be incorrect.

NASA’s UAP study specifically identified accurate acquisition times, sensor locations and instrument metadata as necessary for meaningful analysis. NASA

Compare direction and elevation, not just proximity

A historical aircraft record may contain latitude, longitude, altitude, speed, heading and observation time.

From these values, software can estimate where the aircraft would have appeared in the witness’s sky.

Two measurements are especially useful:

Azimuth describes the compass direction from the observer towards the aircraft. For example, 90° represents east and 270° represents west.

Elevation describes the angle above the horizon. An elevation of 0° is at the horizon, while 90° is directly overhead.

Together, these measurements provide a more meaningful comparison than geographical distance alone.

Suppose a witness reports a bright object approximately 15° above the western horizon. Historical data show two aircraft operating in the area.

One aircraft is relatively nearby but would have appeared high in the southern sky. The other is much farther away but would have appeared approximately 15° above the western horizon.

The second aircraft is the stronger candidate, despite being more distant.

This is an illustrative example rather than a reconstruction of a documented sighting. It demonstrates why matching the witness’s line of sight is more important than simply identifying the nearest flight.

Aircraft altitude must also be handled carefully. Flight trackers may report barometric altitude, which is based on atmospheric pressure, rather than the aircraft’s exact height above the terrain. For precise low-elevation comparisons, investigators may need pressure corrections, terrain elevation and the aircraft’s actual geometric altitude.

Reconstruct movement across the whole sighting

A single matching position is rarely enough.

An aircraft may pass through the reported direction for a few seconds without explaining an object that remained visible for ten minutes.

A stronger identification compares the full sequence of observations.

For example, an investigator might reconstruct whether a candidate aircraft:

  1. First appeared in the reported direction.
  2. Remained close to the reported position during the initial observation.
  3. Began moving sideways at the time the witness described.
  4. Changed its orientation in a way consistent with the observed lights.
  5. Disappeared from view at approximately the reported time.

Where video is available, the reconstructed aircraft position can be compared with the object’s position in successive frames.

Fixed landmarks, stars or buildings can help establish the camera’s orientation and field of view.

A particularly persuasive result occurs when an aircraft’s predicted position matches several successive observations without requiring arbitrary changes to the witness’s location or timing.

Research using OpenSky Network flight data has shown that recorded aircraft trajectories can contain irregular sampling and gaps. Investigators should therefore distinguish measured positions from positions estimated between measurements. MDPI

Which historical flight databases are most useful?

Different flight-tracking services collect and present aviation information in different ways. Some are convenient for visual playback, while others are better suited to detailed historical analysis.

The main distinction is between public flight-tracking archives and official air-traffic surveillance records.

Public services are often the practical starting point for a civilian UFO investigation. Official records may provide stronger or complementary evidence, but are not always accessible.

Data source Investigative value Important limitation
Flightradar24 Visual flight playback, aircraft identification and historical route checks Historical access depends on subscription and available coverage
ADS-B Exchange Aircraft position histories, including some flights hidden by other services Only aircraft signals actually received or otherwise supported by its tracking methods can appear
OpenSky Network Research-oriented historical aircraft positions and trajectory analysis Historical access, geographical coverage and data completeness vary
Official air-traffic surveillance records Potential corroboration from operational radar and surveillance systems Access may be restricted, and records have retention limits

Flightradar24 combines aircraft broadcasts with other flight information and supports historical investigation through playback and airport-history features. Its published subscription information describes different historical access periods for different account levels. Flightradar24

ADS-B Exchange maintains a substantial historical archive. Its published documentation describes archived aircraft-position snapshots at five-second intervals from May 2020, with earlier data at lower sampling frequency. Detailed individual aircraft traces are also available through its data products, subject to access arrangements. ADS-B Exchange

OpenSky Network provides an important research dataset. A 2021 peer-reviewed study in Earth System Science Data documented the network’s historical coverage and explained how receiver availability affects the completeness of reconstructed flights. ESSD

What is ADS-B, and why does it matter?

Automatic Dependent Surveillance–Broadcast, or ADS-B, is a technology through which equipped aircraft transmit information including their position and altitude.

Ground-based receivers collect these signals, allowing tracking networks to reconstruct aircraft movements.

Some services also use multilateration, usually shortened to MLAT. This estimates an aircraft’s position from the arrival times of transponder signals at several receivers.

The distinction matters because an aircraft may transmit information that does not directly include its geographical coordinates, yet still be trackable through MLAT if sufficient receivers detect it.

ADS-B Exchange explains that its MLAT coverage depends on multiple receivers detecting the same aircraft. Older transponder configurations may not provide the information necessary for its public tracking system. ADS-B Exchange Help Center

A missing ADS-B position is therefore not equivalent to a confirmed absence of aircraft.

Why one database may show an aircraft another misses

Flight-tracking networks differ in receiver locations, processing methods, data sources and display policies.

A service may show an aircraft using estimated positions after direct reception has been lost. Another may display only positions supported by received tracking data.

Flightradar24 has explained that it can estimate aircraft positions beyond areas of direct coverage, using information such as the aircraft’s previous heading, speed and expected route. These estimates can become inaccurate if an aircraft changes course. Flightradar24 Blog

ADS-B Exchange states that its displayed aircraft positions are based on received data rather than interpolated flight paths. ADS-B Exchange Help Center

For a UFO investigation, this difference affects the strength of a match.

An aircraft position directly recorded at the sighting time is generally stronger evidence than a position extrapolated across a substantial tracking gap.

Where possible, investigators should preserve the source’s original timestamps, reported positions and any indication that a displayed track contains estimated segments.

What did AARO’s commercial aircraft case reveal?

One of the clearest documented examples of flight-track identification comes from a 2021 incident over military airspace in the western United States.

Military personnel reported unusual lights that appeared to present a possible intrusion into restricted airspace.

The objects were observed through infrared equipment, and their appearance initially raised questions about their identity and distance.

In its [Western United States case resolution]aaro.milOpen source on aaro.mil., dated 8 May 2023, AARO concluded that the observed objects were almost certainly commercial aircraft travelling along established air corridors.

The office reported that the aircraft were much farther from the observing platform than initially estimated, with relevant aircraft up to approximately 300 nautical miles away. That is roughly 556 kilometres. AARO

How the identification was made

AARO compared the observations with additional data, including air-traffic information.

The agency reported strong correlations between the objects’observed positions and specific commercial aircraft travelling along established routes.

The distance finding was particularly important.

If investigators assume that a distant object is close to the observer, its apparent movement can suggest an implausibly high speed. Correcting the distance changes the interpretation of its motion.

AARO also attributed apparent changes in the objects’shapes to sensor vibration and autofocus effects.

The agency’s public imagery page describes three objects in the released video as distant commercial aircraft whose radar tracks aligned with the observed points. The case-resolution document refers more broadly to an initial report of five lights, so the initial witness account and the particular objects analysed in the published imagery should not be treated as identical descriptions. AARO

The case demonstrates three important principles.

First, an object’s apparent position in a sensor image does not reveal its distance automatically.

Second, independently recorded aircraft movements can provide a convincing explanation even when the observed objects initially appear unfamiliar.

Third, sensor behaviour must be examined alongside the flight records. A matching aircraft track may explain the object’s location and motion, while camera or sensor effects explain apparent changes in shape.

What the case does not establish

The AARO finding is a specific case resolution, not proof that every unidentified light is an aircraft.

It involved evidence and analytical resources beyond what a civilian investigator may be able to obtain from a commercial tracking website.

The public documentation supports AARO’s reported conclusion, but does not provide every underlying operational record needed for a fully independent public reconstruction.

Its wider lesson is methodological: the strength of the identification comes from agreement between the observed geometry, flight information and sensor analysis, rather than from the mere presence of aircraft somewhere in the region.

What if no aircraft appears in the historical records?

An empty flight-tracking map is an important observation, but its meaning depends on how complete the available data were.

There are several reasons why an aircraft might not appear in a historical search.

Limited receiver coverage. Ground receivers need to detect aircraft transmissions. Low-flying aircraft may be obscured by terrain, buildings or the curvature of the Earth. Remote regions and some offshore areas can have poorer coverage.

Aircraft equipment and operating mode. Not every aircraft transmits position information in a form that a particular public tracking network can display. Some military and other specialist operations may also be absent.

Privacy and identification restrictions. Certain tracking services limit the information they display. In the United States, the FAA’s Privacy ICAO Address programme allows eligible aircraft to use temporary identifiers, making their ownership harder to establish through public broadcasts. This does not necessarily remove their positions from all tracking networks.

Historical archive limitations. A service may have tracked an aircraft at the time without retaining an accessible record for the date being investigated.

Incomplete or inaccurate records. Tracking data can contain gaps, incorrect identifiers, stale positions and other errors.

These limitations are documented by the FAA, ADS-B Exchange and research using historical aviation datasets. Federal Aviation Administration

The geographical issue is particularly significant. A 2025 OpenSky research report described how terrestrial ADS-B coverage remains limited over oceans and remote areas, motivating the addition of satellite-based aircraft position messages to improve trajectory reconstruction. arXiv

A sighting over a well-covered metropolitan area may therefore be easier to investigate through public flight data than an equivalent report over remote terrain or open water.

Does an empty map rule out commercial aircraft?

Not automatically.

The investigator must first establish whether the relevant database had reliable coverage at the location, altitude and time in question.

One practical check is to examine whether other known aircraft were successfully tracked in the same area during the surrounding period.

If several ordinary flights appear with continuous tracks, that provides some evidence that the tracking network was functioning. It does not prove that every aircraft was visible.

A second check is to compare multiple independent services.

If several datasets show no matching aircraft, and their coverage is demonstrably good, the ordinary-aircraft explanation becomes less persuasive.

However, apparent agreement between services is not necessarily independent confirmation. Two websites may depend partly on the same receivers or underlying data.

A 2026 analysis of US flight surveillance datasets found meaningful differences in geographical coverage and the flights represented by different sources. The researchers concluded that combining datasets can help overcome limitations in individual systems. Sage Journals

Where an incident is sufficiently significant, investigators may seek official air-traffic records. The FAA states that its radar and ADS-B surveillance information is retained for at least six months, although access to specific records is not guaranteed. Federal Aviation Administration

The appropriate conclusion after an unsuccessful search is therefore usually:

No matching aircraft was identified in the available historical records.

That is materially different from claiming that no aircraft was present.

How can AI improve historical flight-track analysis?

AI can make aircraft identification faster by automating the comparison between witness observations and large collections of historical aircraft positions.

Its most useful role is not to decide whether a sighting is extraordinary. It is to identify plausible conventional explanations and show how well each candidate fits the evidence.

A practical investigation can be organised around a small number of evidence-based operations.

Build a time-and-direction search

The system first converts the witness account into structured information.

For example, a report might describe a bright white light seen from a garden at 21:15, initially towards the south-west, remaining almost stationary for several minutes before moving west.

An automated workflow can search the surrounding flight records, allowing for uncertainty in the reported time and direction.

Rather than retrieving only aircraft directly above the witness, it can examine a much wider region and calculate which aircraft would have appeared in the relevant part of the sky.

This avoids a common investigative error: excluding distant aircraft simply because their ground tracks were not close to the witness’s location.

Rank candidates by agreement with the observation

Once potential aircraft have been identified, software can compare their predicted appearances with the reported sighting.

Useful comparisons include:

Comparison What it helps establish
Time overlap Whether the aircraft was present during the observation
Viewing direction Whether it appeared in the reported part of the sky
Elevation Whether its apparent height matches the report
Angular movement Whether its apparent speed and direction agree
Duration Whether it remained visible for the reported period
Aircraft orientation Whether its lights could explain reported colour or brightness changes
Data quality Whether the match relies on measured positions or substantial estimates

A candidate matching the time, direction and full movement sequence should generally receive more attention than one matching only the time.

However, numerical confidence scores require care.

A system should not assign a 95% identification probability simply because five out of six characteristics agree. The reliability of each observation, the number of competing aircraft and the completeness of the tracking data all affect the result.

NASA’s independent study highlighted the potential of AI and machine learning for comparing UAP observations with contextual datasets, while stressing that data quality, calibration and appropriate metadata are prerequisites for meaningful conclusions. NASA Science

Keep the reasoning visible

An AI-generated identification should be inspectable.

For each proposed aircraft, the case file should preserve its reported identifier, data source, relevant timestamps, recorded positions and reconstructed viewing angles.

The analysis should also distinguish:

  • Confirmed data: Aircraft positions and timestamps actually present in the source records.
  • Calculated relationships: Bearings, elevation angles and apparent movement derived from those records.
  • Candidate explanations: Aircraft that could plausibly produce the observation.
  • Unresolved discrepancies: Details of the sighting that the proposed aircraft does not explain.

This separation prevents a plausible correlation from quietly becoming a claimed identification.

AI can also help identify contradictions. For instance, a candidate might fit the initial viewing direction but leave the field of view several minutes before the witness reported the object’s disappearance.

That mismatch should reduce confidence, not be concealed by a favourable overall score.

When is an aircraft identification convincing?

A convincing identification requires more than an aircraft being present at approximately the right time.

The strongest cases demonstrate a consistent match between independently recorded flight behaviour and the witness’s observation.

The evidential strength can be described in practical terms.

Assessment Appropriate finding
Strong identification Recorded aircraft positions closely match the sighting’s time, direction, elevation and movement, with no substantial unexplained conflict
Plausible aircraft explanation An aircraft fits several important features, but some observations or records are too uncertain for a firm identification
Weak correlation An aircraft was nearby, but the available information does not establish that it occupied the reported line of sight
Aircraft explanation contradicted Reliable observations conflict substantially with the proposed aircraft’s position or movement
Unresolved Available records and witness evidence are insufficient to identify or confidently exclude an aircraft

These are descriptive investigative categories, not formally standardised AARO classifications.

A particularly strong match might involve a time-stamped video showing a light moving past identifiable stars or buildings, with a recorded aircraft following the same apparent path at the same time.

A weaker match might involve a witness recalling an approximate time and a vague direction, while several aircraft were operating in the wider region.

The latter situation may support an ordinary explanation, but does not justify naming a specific aircraft as the source.

The difference matters because false certainty can distort a case file in either direction. Investigators can incorrectly dismiss a genuine unresolved observation by forcing a weak aircraft match, or incorrectly describe an ordinary aircraft sighting as anomalous because a single tracking search returned no result.

Can flight tracks rule out an aircraft explanation completely?

Historical flight records can sometimes make a particular aircraft explanation highly unlikely, especially when the observation is well documented and the proposed aircraft’s trajectory is incompatible with it.

They are much less capable of proving that no aircraft of any kind could have been responsible.

A well-recorded sighting may contain information that contradicts every aircraft visible in the available databases. That is meaningful evidence against those specific candidates.

It does not automatically exclude untracked aircraft, incomplete surveillance records or errors in the original observations.

The strength of a negative finding depends on the quality of both sides of the comparison: the sighting evidence and the available aviation records.

For example, a clear, correctly timed video with fixed visual reference points provides a stronger basis for excluding tracked aircraft than an account reconstructed several weeks later.

Likewise, continuous surveillance from multiple sources is more informative than a single incomplete playback map.

Even when a flight-track comparison fails to identify an object, the investigation may still have made substantial progress. It can establish which ordinary aircraft explanations were tested, which were inconsistent with the observations and which could not be evaluated because of missing information.

That distinction is essential for an evidence-led UFO case file.

What should the final case file say?

Historical flight-track analysis is most useful when its results are reported with enough detail for another investigator to understand the identification.

A well-supported finding should identify the relevant aircraft where possible, specify the observation period, describe the predicted viewing direction and explain how the aircraft’s movement compares with the witness account.

It should also disclose significant gaps, such as uncertain witness timing, estimated aircraft positions or incomplete receiver coverage.

The AARO western United States case demonstrates what a successful investigation can achieve: objects initially considered unidentified were linked to distant commercial aircraft through a combination of flight information, viewing geometry and sensor analysis.

But the case also shows why simple visual impressions can be misleading. Objects that appear nearby may be hundreds of kilometres away, and changes in apparent shape may originate in the observing equipment rather than the object itself.

The decisive question is not whether an aircraft was flying nearby, but whether its reconstructed appearance matches what was actually observed.

When historical tracks, witness direction, timing and movement agree, an aircraft identification can be compelling. When they conflict, that explanation should be rejected or weakened. When the records are missing, the result remains uncertain.

This evidence-based distinction allows AI-assisted UFO investigations to resolve ordinary aircraft sightings efficiently while preserving genuinely unresolved cases for further examination.
