How Do You Turn a UFO Report Into Evidence?

A useful UFO investigation starts by preserving witness accounts, checking photographs and reconstructing exactly when and where the sighting occurred.

Published 22 Oct 2026

On this page

  • What to record from witnesses: exact words, dates, times, locations, viewing directions and uncertainty
  • Reconstructing the sighting timeline and separating observed behaviour from witness interpretations
  • Checking original photographs, video metadata and camera effects without introducing artificial details

Introduction

A reliable UFO sighting case file turns an unusual observation into a documented event that can be investigated, checked and reassessed. The essential steps are to preserve the witness’s original account, establish exactly when and where the sighting happened, reconstruct what was observed, secure any photographs or video, and distinguish verified facts from estimates and interpretations.

The objective is not to prove or disprove an extraordinary explanation. It is to create a trustworthy record that allows investigators to test ordinary explanations, identify contradictions and recognise genuinely unresolved questions.

Artificial intelligence (AI) can help extract information, organise timelines and identify missing evidence. However, its findings are only as dependable as the original material.

This approach reflects the work of France’s official UFO investigation service, GEIPAN, which uses witness questionnaires, supporting evidence and documented investigations. NASA’s 2023 UAP study similarly emphasised standardised observations, reliable sensor information and better-quality data. GEIPAN

How do you build a UFO case file step by step?

A case file should be organised around one specific observation, even when several people witnessed it. Each statement, recording and contextual finding should remain traceable to its source.

The following sequence provides a practical workflow.

  1. Create a case record. Assign a unique reference number and record when the report was received, who received it and whether the observation is first-hand.
  2. Preserve the initial testimony. Retain the witness’s original description without rewriting unusual language or correcting apparent contradictions.
  3. Collect essential sighting details. Record the date, time, location, duration, viewing direction, appearance, movement and observing conditions.
  4. Reconstruct the event. Establish a chronological sequence showing when the object appeared, what happened during the sighting and how the observation ended.
  5. Secure original evidence. Obtain photographs, video, audio, sketches and instrument records in their earliest available forms.
  6. Check evidence quality. Examine timestamps, camera characteristics, missing footage, uncertain estimates and possible recording artefacts.
  7. Document uncertainties and contradictions. Clearly distinguish direct observations, later recollections, investigator calculations and possible explanations.
  8. Prepare the file for verification. Produce a structured summary that can support subsequent checks against aircraft, astronomical objects, satellites, weather and other possible causes.

GEIPAN’s published methodology provides a useful institutional model: it begins with testimony and case-file creation before progressing to analysis, investigation and classification. GEIPAN

The order matters. Beginning with an attractive explanation can influence subsequent questioning, while preserving the account first allows competing explanations to be examined against the same underlying record.

What information should every UFO report contain?

A useful report records both the event itself and the circumstances under which it was observed.

The most important distinction is between details that can be independently verified and those that depend on the witness’s perception or memory.

These fields should not all be treated as equally reliable. A precise date supported by an original recording may be stronger than a remembered time supplied months later. Similarly, a compass bearing measured during a reconstruction is different from a direction recalled from memory.

NASA’s 2023 independent study highlighted the limitations of observations without reliable timing, location, sensor information and calibration. Such gaps can prevent researchers from making firm conclusions even where video evidence exists. NASA Science

Record uncertainty rather than filling gaps

An apparently incomplete record can still be valuable if its limitations are clear.

For example, a witness might report an observation at approximately 21:30, but only remember that it occurred sometime between 21:15 and 21:45. Recording 21:30 as an exact timestamp would create misleading precision.

A stronger case file would distinguish the original estimate from the possible interval and record how the time was established.

Use the same approach for direction, duration, apparent size and distance. A missing value should remain unknown rather than being replaced with an AI-generated estimate.

How should witness testimony be collected?

Witness interviews are often the most sensitive stage of UFO evidence collection. The investigator must obtain detailed information without unintentionally shaping the account.

The UK College of Policing’s guidance on non-leading questioning [college.police.uk]college.police.ukCollege of Policing Non-leading approach to questioningCollege of Policing Non-leading approach to questioningPublished: November 24, 2020 explains that suggestive questions can cause witnesses to report details they did not originally observe. Although the guidance concerns investigative interviewing generally, the same risk applies when questioning UFO witnesses. College of Policing

Preserve the witness’s exact words

Begin with an open invitation such as:

Please describe everything you remember seeing, from the moment you first noticed it until the sighting ended.

Allow the witness to recount the event before introducing structured questions.

The initial statement should preserve unusual descriptions, emotional reactions and uncertainty. Corrections or clarifications should be stored separately rather than silently replacing the first version.

Consider the difference between these statements:

  • Original observation: “I saw a bright white light moving towards the rooftops.”
  • Witness interpretation: “It looked as though a spacecraft was coming down.”
  • Investigator’s inference: “The object’s apparent elevation decreased during the sighting.”

These are not interchangeable. The first describes what was seen, the second communicates the witness’s interpretation, and the third attempts to express the observation in measurable terms.

A reliable file keeps all three distinct.

Ask neutral, specific questions

After the uninterrupted account, useful questions include:

  • Where were you standing when you first noticed the object?
  • What were you doing immediately before the sighting?
  • Which direction were you facing?
  • What could you see around or behind the object?
  • Did its appearance change while you watched?
  • What made you look away or lose sight of it?
  • How did you establish the time and duration?
  • Did you discuss the sighting or see online reports before giving this account?

Avoid questions that assume a particular behaviour. For example,”What did it look like when it accelerated?”is inappropriate unless acceleration has already been described.

An alternative is:”Did its apparent movement change? If so, what did you notice?”

It is equally important not to imply that a particular answer is expected.

Keep independent witness accounts separate

Several witnesses can provide valuable corroboration, but only if their accounts are meaningfully independent.

People who discuss an observation together may unintentionally adopt one another’s details. The College of Policing’s research-based guidance recommends obtaining initial accounts separately because discussion between witnesses can introduce information that an individual did not personally observe. College of Policing

For a UFO case, each witness should ideally describe the event independently before being shown other accounts or proposed explanations.

Record whether witnesses knew each other, where they stood and what they discussed before reporting the sighting.

Three people repeating a description developed during a group conversation do not provide the same evidential value as three independently documented observations.

How do you reconstruct the sighting timeline?

A timeline converts a narrative into an ordered sequence of observations.

This is especially useful when witnesses describe sudden changes of direction, disappearance, hovering or unusual apparent speeds.

Instead of treating the sighting as one continuous event, break it into identifiable moments.

Consider a hypothetical observation from a residential street:

Approximate time Recorded event Evidence status
21:14 Witness notices a bright light above nearby rooftops Witness recollection
21:15 Light appears to move steadily towards the east Witness estimate
21:16 Witness begins recording Potentially verifiable from original video
21:17 Light becomes dimmer and disappears Witness account and video, subject to verification
21:19 Witness sends a message describing the sighting Potentially verifiable from message records

This timeline does not identify the object. It establishes which details may be tested and which remain dependent on memory.

The next step is to align witness descriptions with recording timestamps and any independently established references.

A crucial distinction is between the time of the observation, the time a recording was created, and the time the witness reported the event. These may differ substantially.

Reconstruct the viewing geometry

Knowing where a witness stood is only half the geographical problem. Investigators must also establish where the witness was looking.

A useful reconstruction records the observer’s position, the direction towards the object, its apparent height above the horizon, and identifiable landmarks.

A compass bearing describes horizontal direction; elevation describes the angle above the horizon. Together, these can constrain the line of sight, although they do not establish the object’s distance.

Photographs of the location taken during daylight can help identify rooftops, trees, hills and other reference points.

A witness who describes an object moving behind a building provides a potentially useful geographical constraint. By contrast, saying that an object disappeared”in the distance”may offer little measurable information.

The observer’s own movement must also be recorded. Turning a corner, travelling in a vehicle or following an object with a camera can change its apparent motion.

A useful real-world illustration comes from the US Department of Defense’s All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO). In its analysis of the Puerto Rico infrared sighting recorded in April 2013, AARO concluded that apparent high speed was attributable to motion parallax, an effect caused by changing positions between the observing sensor and the objects. AARO

The investigative lesson is straightforward: apparent motion cannot reliably be translated into real-world speed without information about the observer, sensor, viewing angle and possible target distance.

How should photographs and video be preserved?

An original recording is one of the most valuable possible additions to a UFO case file, but it is not automatically proof of an unusual object.

A camera records incoming light through a particular optical and electronic system. Its output can be affected by exposure, focus, stabilisation, movement, compression and later editing.

The first priority is therefore preservation, not enhancement.

The US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) recommends documenting digital evidence provenance, maintaining its integrity, using appropriate cryptographic hashes and retaining secure copies. Although its guidance is intended for formal evidence management, the principles also apply to civilian sighting records. NIST

Preserve the earliest available version

Request the original photograph or video directly from the recording device wherever possible.

Social media uploads, messaging applications and video-sharing platforms may introduce compression or other changes. A downloaded version should therefore not automatically be treated as the camera original.

For each file, retain:

  • Original filename, format and file size.
  • Source device and, where available, camera model.
  • Acquisition date and method.
  • Embedded metadata and any available recording settings.
  • Cryptographic hash, preferably SHA-256, calculated when the file is acquired.
  • A record of any later transfers, conversions or alterations.

A cryptographic hash is a digital fingerprint calculated from a file’s contents. Matching hashes can demonstrate that two files contain identical data, but they cannot establish that the scene itself was genuine.

Store the preserved original separately from working copies. Any cropping, enhancement or frame extraction should be documented and performed on copies.

The Scientific Working Group on Digital Evidence (SWGDE) makes this distinction explicit in its image-authentication guidance: maintaining file integrity is not the same as proving that the scene depicted is authentic. SWGDE

Check metadata without assuming it is infallible

Photographs may contain Exchangeable Image File Format (EXIF) metadata. This can include camera model, exposure settings, image dimensions, recording time and sometimes GPS coordinates.

Video files may contain similar information, but their metadata structures vary.

The examiner should establish what the metadata actually says, which software produced it, and whether the values agree with other evidence.

For example, a video timestamp may reflect a camera’s internal clock rather than a reliably synchronised time source. Time-zone information may also be absent or handled differently by software.

SWGDE’s digital video authentication guidance warns that metadata can be altered without changing video playback and should not be relied upon in isolation. SWGDE

This creates an important investigative distinction:

A timestamp extracted from a file is a recorded value, not necessarily the verified time of the sighting.

If a witness reports that a phone clock was incorrect, retain both the original metadata and any documented correction.

How do you recognise camera effects and misleading images?

Some of the most striking UFO footage contains visual features that are produced or exaggerated by the camera rather than by the observed object.

Investigators should examine the complete recording before interpreting individual frames.

Potential issues include:

Focus and blur. A distant point of light can appear as a large disc, ring or irregular shape when the camera is out of focus. Such an image does not necessarily reveal the object’s physical outline.

Exposure and saturation. Bright lights can overwhelm a camera sensor, obscuring detail and distorting apparent shape or brightness.

Camera movement and stabilisation. Hand movements, digital stabilisation and changing zoom can affect the apparent movement of an object against the background.

Reflections and lens effects. Internal reflections, glare and lens flare may create luminous shapes that appear to move in unusual ways.

Compression artefacts. Video encoding can produce blocks, smearing, missing detail or artificial-looking boundaries around moving objects.

Rolling-shutter distortion. Some cameras record different parts of an image at slightly different moments, potentially distorting the appearance of rapidly moving subjects.

These possibilities should be examined rather than assumed. A technical artefact is a candidate explanation only when the image characteristics support it.

AARO’s published analysis of infrared footage from South Asia illustrates the importance of examining sensor effects. The office assessed that an object was likely a commercial aircraft and that an apparent trailing wake was caused by video compression rather than a physical phenomenon. AARO

Why AI enhancement can weaken evidence

AI-based image enhancement can be useful for exploration, but it introduces a serious risk when treated as evidence.

Some systems generate plausible detail to make an image appear sharper. This can create apparent edges, textures or structures that were not reliably captured in the original recording.

A processed image showing windows, panels or a sharply defined outline does not establish that those features existed.

The safest procedure is to retain the original recording, label every derivative, record the processing method and avoid drawing factual conclusions from details introduced solely by generative enhancement.

Traditional adjustments such as brightness or contrast correction may also change how a feature appears. They should remain reproducible and clearly documented.

The SWGDE’s forensic image-analysis guidance addresses the effects of compression, alterations and processing on interpretation, reinforcing the need to distinguish analytical products from underlying evidence. SWGDE

How can AI turn a raw report into a structured case file?

AI is particularly useful when an investigator receives a long narrative containing scattered times, locations, descriptions and uncertainties.

A language model can extract candidate information into predefined fields, identify contradictions and flag missing details.

For example, the statement:

I was driving home just after nine. I stopped near the railway bridge and saw three orange lights towards the north. They seemed to stay still for perhaps two minutes, then one disappeared. My passenger took a video.

could produce a structured preliminary record with the following distinctions:

Extracted field Preliminary value Verification needed
Time Shortly after 21:00 Exact date, time zone and clock reference
Location Near a railway bridge Specific bridge and observer coordinates
Object count Three lights initially Confirm whether all were visible together
Colour Orange Confirm lighting and atmospheric conditions
Direction Approximately north Establish bearing using landmarks
Motion Apparently stationary Determine whether observer or camera was moving
Duration Approximately two minutes Check recording and witness estimate
Supporting evidence Passenger’s video Obtain original file and separate testimony

This demonstrates the difference between extracting information and validating it.

AI can identify that the report contains three lights, but it cannot independently establish that three physical objects existed. Likewise, it should not invent coordinates because the witness mentioned a railway bridge.

Keep the original account linked to extracted fields

A trustworthy implementation should retain the source passage behind each extracted field.

If the AI records”three orange lights”, an investigator should be able to find the exact witness statement that supports it.

The case record should also distinguish information entered directly by witnesses, information extracted by software, later corrections and investigator-derived measurements.

This creates an audit trail and prevents automated summaries from gradually replacing the original account.

Useful AI checks include identifying inconsistent times, conflicting directions, unexplained changes in object count and claims of speed unsupported by distance measurements.

However, the system should flag these as questions rather than silently resolving them.

NASA’s 2023 UAP study emphasised the importance of standardisation and clear scientific evidence thresholds when using automated methods. NASA Science

How do you separate unusual observations from extraordinary claims?

A reliable case file does not need to decide immediately whether an object was an aircraft, drone, balloon, astronomical body or something unexplained.

It needs to establish which observations can be supported.

Consider a witness who reports:

The object accelerated from a standstill to an impossible speed.

That statement contains several different claims.

The object appeared stationary. Its apparent position then changed. The witness interpreted the change as acceleration. Finally, the witness concluded that the speed exceeded ordinary capabilities.

Only the earlier portions may be directly supported by testimony or imagery. The stronger physical conclusion requires further measurements, particularly reliable distance and timing.

Without known distance, angular movement across the sky cannot be converted confidently into actual speed.

GEIPAN’s methodology explicitly recognises the difficulty of estimating distance and speed for unfamiliar aerial objects. It also distinguishes the consistency of available information from the remaining strangeness of a case after investigation. GEIPAN

An effective case file should therefore classify individual statements rather than assigning one blanket credibility judgement to the entire witness account.

A practical distinction is:

Evidence status Meaning
Directly reported Described by a witness but not necessarily independently verified
Independently verified Supported by a checkable source or measurement
Estimated Approximate value with known or unknown error
Inferred Derived through reasoning or calculation
Contradicted Conflicts with another relevant source
Unresolved Cannot currently be established from available evidence

This prevents an investigator from treating an interpretation as a measurement merely because it appears in a formal report.

What makes a UFO case file genuinely reliable?

Reliability depends on the quality, independence and traceability of the material, not simply how much information has been collected.

A case supported by one original recording with confirmed provenance and accurately reconstructed viewing conditions may be stronger than a case containing dozens of reposted and speculative commentary.

Four characteristics are particularly important.

Traceability. Every significant fact can be connected to an original statement, recording, measurement or external record.

Reproducibility. Another investigator can understand how timestamps were corrected, directions established and media examined.

Independence. Corroborating accounts or recordings are evaluated for whether they provide genuinely separate information.

Transparent limitations. Missing data, estimated quantities, recording gaps and alternative interpretations remain visible throughout the investigation.

These principles are illustrated by GEIPAN’s published case archive. Its 14 May 2022 Montluel investigation contains a structured sighting record, recorded local times, witness descriptions and supporting documents including a technical questionnaire and reconstruction material. The case remained classified as unidentified after investigation despite its documented evidence. GEIPAN

This illustrates an important distinction: a detailed case file can support serious investigation without necessarily producing a definitive identification.

Conversely, an unresolved classification does not establish an extraordinary origin.

What mistakes most often undermine a case file?

Even an apparently careful investigation can become unreliable when early mistakes remain hidden.

Several errors are especially consequential.

Treating approximate times as exact. A false level of precision can create convincing but incorrect matches with aircraft or satellite records.

Confusing distance with apparent size. An unfamiliar object can appear large or nearby without enough information to determine its physical dimensions.

Combining witness accounts too early. Group discussion can make descriptions seem more consistent than the original observations were.

Using altered media as the primary evidence. Cropped, enhanced or compressed footage may hide features needed to understand the observation.

Assuming metadata proves authenticity. Embedded timestamps and GPS information can support provenance checks but may be missing, incorrect or altered.

Ignoring the observer’s movement. Apparent hovering, acceleration and changing direction can be influenced by the observer’s position or camera tracking.

Allowing AI to repair missing details. Automatically generated bearings, timestamps or descriptions can become false facts if not clearly labelled as estimates.

Treating a failed identification as proof of an anomaly. Inadequate information and genuinely unusual evidence are different outcomes.

A practical safeguard is to maintain two versions of the case record: a preserved evidence record and a working analytical record. The former contains original materials and their acquisition history; the latter contains extracts, calculations, hypotheses and revisions.

This separation makes it possible to revisit a disputed conclusion without losing sight of what was actually supplied.

When is a case file ready for further investigation?

A case file is ready for systematic comparison when the investigator can identify the observation’s time and location with useful uncertainty bounds, reconstruct the reported viewing direction, describe the sequence of events and establish what original evidence exists.

It does not have to be complete. Missing information must simply be documented clearly enough that later searches do not pretend to greater accuracy than the evidence allows.

Before proceeding, confirm that the file answers five practical questions:

  1. What was reported? The witness’s words and the directly observed characteristics are preserved.
  2. When and where did it happen? The observation has a usable temporal and geographical reference, including uncertainty.
  3. How was it observed? Witness movement, viewing conditions and recording devices are documented.
  4. What evidence survives? Original or earliest-available material is identified, preserved and linked to its source.
  5. What remains uncertain? Contradictions, missing measurements and unverified interpretations are explicit.

Once those conditions are satisfied, AI-assisted workflows can compare the case with aviation movements, astronomical positions, satellite activity, weather and historical reports without losing the evidential basis of the original sighting.

GEIPAN’s classification framework makes a useful final distinction: cases may be identified, probably identified, insufficiently documented or remain unidentified following investigation. Its process also allows cases to be revisited when new evidence emerges. GEIPAN

The central value of a reliable UFO case file is therefore not that it guarantees an answer. It is that the evidence remains clear enough to test an explanation, challenge a conclusion or reopen the investigation when better information becomes available.
