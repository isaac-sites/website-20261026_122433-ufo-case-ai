How Can You Reconstruct a UFO Sighting Accurately?

A UFO sighting timeline can reveal what happened and when, provided uncertain times, viewing directions and recording gaps remain visible.

Published 22 Oct 2026

On this page

  • Building a sequence from first appearance to disappearance using witness observations
  • Checking reported times, viewing directions and apparent movement against recordings and landmarks
  • Using AI to organise conflicting timestamps and identify gaps without inventing missing details

Introduction

Reconstructing a UFO sighting accurately means arranging every reported observation, photograph, video clip and independent record into a chronological sequence while keeping uncertain times and missing evidence visible. The aim is not to produce a perfectly continuous story. It is to establish what can be verified, what witnesses remember and which parts of the event remain unknown.

A reliable reconstruction starts with the first appearance, follows changes in position or behaviour, and ends with the last confirmed observation. Investigators then compare reported times with recording metadata, visible landmarks and other time-stamped evidence.

Artificial intelligence (AI) can organise these details, identify contradictions and suggest where to look for missing information. It cannot legitimately invent events between observations.

This distinction is central to NASA’s 2023 study of unidentified anomalous phenomena (UAP), which emphasised accurate timing, sensor metadata and well-calibrated observations as foundations for meaningful investigation. NASA Science

How do you reconstruct a UFO sighting step by step?

Begin with a working timeline rather than an explanation of what the object might have been. A witness who reports seeing a bright object at approximately 21:15, filming it briefly and watching it disappear has already provided several potentially useful events. However, those events may have different levels of timing accuracy.

The following six-step process turns such a report into a timeline that can be checked and revised.

  1. Preserve the original account. Record the witness’s exact description, including uncertain wording such as”about ten minutes”or”shortly after sunset”. Do not replace approximate statements with exact times.

  2. Divide the sighting into observable stages. Identify first appearance, changes in brightness or movement, interruptions, recording periods, and the final observation.

  3. Collect independent time references. Examine original photographs, video files, messages, telephone records and other available evidence that might establish when particular stages occurred.

  4. Check the viewing geometry. Record where the witness stood, which direction they faced, where the object appeared relative to landmarks and whether the observer or camera moved.

  5. Reconcile timestamps and uncertainties. Convert times to a consistent reference, check device clocks and distinguish measured durations from estimates.

  6. Build an annotated timeline. Give each event a time or time window, an evidence source, an uncertainty assessment and a note explaining any disagreement.

France’s official UFO investigation service, GEIPAN, provides a useful model for this evidence-first approach. Its investigation process centres on direct witness testimony, supported where possible by sketches, photographs, videos and other observations. GEIPAN

Record separate stages, not one continuous movement

A sighting often contains several distinct observations that should not automatically be treated as uninterrupted activity.

For example, a witness may first notice a stationary light, look away to fetch a telephone, begin filming, observe apparent sideways movement and finally lose sight of the light behind a building.

The interval spent retrieving the telephone is important. The investigator cannot assume the light remained stationary during that period, even if it appears in roughly the same part of the sky when filming begins.

A useful reconstruction therefore distinguishes:

  • First detection: When the witness first noticed the phenomenon, not necessarily when it first appeared.
  • Initial behaviour: Its reported position, appearance and movement.
  • Changes: Turning, brightening, dimming, splitting or apparent acceleration.
  • Observation interruptions: Moments when the witness looked away or the object became obscured.
  • Recorded intervals: Periods directly supported by photographs, video or instruments.
  • Last detection: The final confirmed sighting, which may not be the moment the phenomenon physically disappeared.

This structure avoids one of the most consequential mistakes in UFO investigations: treating the absence of observation as evidence that nothing changed.

How should uncertain times appear in the timeline?

A precise-looking timestamp is not necessarily an accurate one. A camera may record seconds correctly while its internal clock is several minutes fast. Conversely, a witness may remember only an approximate clock time but accurately describe the order of events.

The practical solution is to store time ranges and their sources, not just single timestamps.

Consider the following fictional example of a sighting reported near a railway station.

Event Reconstructed time Evidence and limitation
First light noticed 21:12–21:15 Witness estimate
Light appears stationary After first detection Witness account only
Telephone recording begins 21:16:08, device time Original video metadata; clock not yet verified
Light moves towards rooftop 18 seconds into recording Visible in video
Light passes behind roofline 43 seconds into recording Visible in video
Witness reports seeing it again Approximately 21:18 No recording
Final sighting 21:18–21:21 Witness estimate

This timeline contains several different kinds of information. The recording establishes an internal sequence of events, but its absolute clock time remains provisional. The witness’s account establishes that another observation was reported after the recorded disappearance, but does not independently verify what happened during the gap.

A defensible timeline preserves these differences instead of smoothing them into a single continuous flight path.

Distinguish clock time from elapsed time

Two separate questions need answering:

What time did the event happen? This requires an accurate date, time zone and clock reference.

How long did the event last? This may be established from video frame timing or estimated from witness recollection.

These measurements should not be confused. A video can reliably establish that an object remained visible for 43 seconds even when the recording device’s clock was incorrectly set.

The US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) specifically addresses clock offsets in its guidance on CCTV video evidence. Its recommended approach involves comparing a recording system’s clock with an external reference and documenting the difference. NIST

For a UFO case, investigators should record the original device time, any measured clock correction and the resulting adjusted time separately.

A simple rule is to retain the original value even after a correction. This allows another investigator to reproduce the calculation.

Use consistent time zones

A sighting at 22:00 in London cannot be compared directly with a flight record at 22:00 UTC without first checking whether British Summer Time was in effect.

The case file should preserve the reported local time and record the corresponding Coordinated Universal Time (UTC), together with the date and applicable offset.

Particular care is needed around daylight-saving changes, when some local clock times can occur twice or not at all.

An unknown time zone should be labelled unknown rather than silently assumed.

How can photographs and video establish the sequence?

Photographs and video often provide the strongest timing anchors in an incomplete UFO report, but only when their technical limitations are understood.

A recording establishes what its camera captured during a particular interval. It does not automatically establish when the entire sighting began, whether the camera was pointed at the same object throughout, or what happened outside the frame.

The Scientific Working Group on Digital Evidence (SWGDE), which publishes forensic guidance on electronic evidence, treats timeline reconstruction as a process of relating video, still images, audio and other records in chronological order. Its guidance also warns that differences between recording systems can cause synchronisation errors. SWGDE

Preserve the original recording

The first task is to obtain the earliest available version of every recording. A file shared through a messaging service may have been compressed, converted or stripped of useful metadata.

Preserve the original separately and examine a working copy. Record its filename, format, resolution, duration and available creation timestamps. Where practical, calculate a cryptographic hash, which acts as a digital fingerprint for checking whether the file changes.

Do not assume that the file creation date is the recording date. Copying, exporting or editing a file can produce new timestamps.

SWGDE’s digital video authentication guidance specifically cautions that metadata can be altered without changing how a video plays. Metadata should therefore be checked against other features of the recording rather than treated as conclusive evidence on its own. SWGDE

Examine frame timing rather than assuming a constant frame rate

A common shortcut is to calculate the time of an event by dividing its frame number by the video’s advertised frame rate.

This can be misleading.

Some digital recordings use variable frame timing, meaning individual frames do not necessarily represent identical intervals. Editing or conversion may also change how frames are displayed.

SWGDE’s technical guidance explains that frame timing must sometimes be reconstructed from information distributed throughout the video file, rather than inferred from a single frame-rate value. SWGDE

For a UFO recording, the practical procedure is to identify the frames where significant changes occur, examine their actual presentation times and check for missing or duplicated frames.

Suppose a light appears to move suddenly between two frames. Before describing this as instantaneous acceleration, investigators should establish whether the recording contains a timing discontinuity, camera movement or a change in tracking.

A genuine movement in the image is an observation. The object’s physical acceleration is a separate inference.

Synchronise recordings using shared events

When two witnesses record the same sighting, their videos may be aligned using an event visible or audible in both.

Potential synchronisation points include a distinctive flash, a change in the object’s brightness, an aircraft crossing the scene or another identifiable occurrence.

For example, suppose two recordings show the same distinctive flash:

  • Camera A records it at 12.4 seconds into its clip.
  • Camera B records it at 7.1 seconds into its clip.
  • The corresponding events are therefore separated by 5.3 seconds on the recordings’relative timelines.

That difference can help align the footage even if neither camera has an accurate clock.

However, the match must be convincing. Two ordinary flashes from separate sources should not be treated as the same event merely because synchronising them produces an attractive explanation.

If a recording is long, investigators should also check for timing drift by comparing another shared event later in the footage.

How do viewing directions and landmarks improve accuracy?

Time alone cannot reconstruct a UFO sighting. The observer’s position and viewing direction are equally important because they determine what part of the sky was visible at each stage.

A statement such as”the object moved from left to right”describes movement across the witness’s field of view, not necessarily movement from west to east.

The interpretation depends on which way the witness was facing and whether they turned their head, walked, drove or moved the camera.

A practical reconstruction should establish three things.

Observer position. Record where the witness stood during each observation. If they moved, create a separate observation position for each stage.

Viewing direction. Record the approximate compass bearing and elevation angle. Azimuth is the horizontal direction measured clockwise from north; elevation is the angle above the horizon.

Landmark relationship. Identify rooftops, trees, chimneys, hills, towers or other fixed features visible near the phenomenon.

A witness who remembers the object passing just above a church spire provides a potentially useful spatial reference, even when they cannot estimate its distance.

Investigators can return to the location, photograph the view and measure the landmark’s approximate bearing. If the original recording includes the same landmark, its position may help reconstruct the camera’s orientation.

However, a single viewing direction generally does not establish distance. An object close to the observer and another much farther away can appear along the same line of sight.

Apparent movement is not necessarily physical speed

This distinction matters particularly when witnesses report extraordinary acceleration.

An object may appear to cross the field of view quickly because the camera is moving, the observer is travelling or the object is much closer than assumed.

The US All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO) examined these issues in its analysis of the January 2015 naval infrared recording known as Go Fast.

The video appeared to show an object travelling rapidly over the ocean. In its February 2025 case assessment, AARO concluded with high confidence that the object did not display anomalous speed. Its analysis incorporated sensor information, viewing geometry, aircraft motion and wind conditions, while acknowledging that the object’s precise position could not be established with high confidence. The object’s identity remained undetermined. AARO

The case illustrates an important lesson for timeline reconstruction: a sequence of recorded positions may be accurate while an initial interpretation of the object’s movement is not.

For a ground-based sighting, investigators should therefore describe movement initially in observational terms, such as”the light shifted approximately ten degrees to the right over six seconds”.

Claims about speed, distance or acceleration should follow only when the necessary measurements support them.

How should investigators handle conflicting witness accounts?

Two witnesses can observe the same event yet remember different start times, durations or movements.

Such differences do not automatically establish dishonesty, nor should they be resolved by choosing the most confident witness.

The better approach is to preserve each account independently and compare them against common reference points.

Suppose one witness estimates that a sighting lasted two minutes while another estimates five. If a video captures 70 seconds, it establishes a minimum recorded interval, not necessarily the total duration.

The witnesses may have noticed the object at different moments, watched it for different periods or estimated elapsed time differently.

The UK’s College of Policing advises investigators to use open questions, avoid suggesting answers and allow witnesses to express uncertainty. Its evidence reviews find that pressuring witnesses to provide more precise answers can reduce accuracy. College of Policing

For a UFO investigation, useful neutral questions include:

  • “What were you doing immediately before you noticed the light?”
  • “What happened next?”
  • “Did you look away at any point?”
  • “What fixed features could you see near it?”
  • “How did you estimate the time?”
  • “Did you discuss the sighting with anyone before making this report?”

These questions help establish chronology without implying that the object must have behaved in a particular way.

Witnesses should ideally provide their first accounts separately, before viewing each other’s footage or discussing possible explanations.

Later recollections can still be valuable, but the case file should identify when they were recorded and whether the witness had encountered additional information.

A disagreement that remains unresolved should appear in the final timeline as a disagreement.

How can AI identify gaps without inventing events?

AI is particularly useful when an investigator has several witness statements, recordings and time references that need to be organised into one coherent record.

The most valuable application is not generating a narrative. It is extracting claims, comparing their timing and showing where the available information fails to support a continuous sequence.

NASA’s 2023 UAP study identified AI and machine learning as potentially useful for analysing and correlating UAP data, while stressing that reliable analysis depends on well-curated observations and appropriate metadata. NASA

A practical AI-assisted timeline workflow can be organised into four stages.

Extract events with traceable sources

An AI system can examine a witness transcript and identify statements that describe a change or event.

For example:

“The light stayed above the houses for a while. Then it flashed twice. I started filming, but it had already begun moving.”

The system might extract three events: apparent stationary observation, two reported flashes and recording beginning after movement had started.

Each extracted event should retain the exact passage that supports it.

The system must not assign exact seconds to the flashes simply because the final timeline requires timestamps.

Identify timing relationships

AI can distinguish absolute times from relative statements.

“At 21:15”is an absolute clock-time claim.”About two minutes later”is a relative interval.”Before I started filming”establishes an order without necessarily establishing a duration.

These distinctions allow software to construct a partial chronology even when many timestamps are unknown.

For example, if the original footage establishes that recording began at 21:16:08, and the witness explicitly says the flashing ended before filming began, the system can place the flashing before that point.

It cannot determine exactly how much earlier the flashing occurred without additional evidence.

Flag contradictions and missing intervals

A useful AI system should produce questions rather than silently resolve incompatible statements.

It might identify that one witness described an object moving east before a recording began, while another described it as stationary during an overlapping interval.

That discrepancy could reflect different observation positions, mistaken timing, multiple objects or imperfect recollection.

The system should retain these possibilities without choosing one prematurely.

It can also identify missing material, such as a recording that begins after the reported movement or a gap between two clips.

The result should make uncertainty easier to investigate, not harder to notice.

Require human approval for inferred events

Automated reconstruction should distinguish at least four types of timeline entries:

Entry type Meaning Example
Directly recorded Visible or audible in original evidence Light crosses a rooftop in video
Witness-reported Described by an observer Light appeared stationary before filming
Calculated Derived from measurements or verified corrections Adjusted camera time
Inferred Suggested by relationships between observations Possible continued movement during a gap

An inferred event should never be presented as if it were directly recorded.

AI-generated descriptions, interpolated video frames and reconstructed movement paths must also be clearly labelled as analytical products.

The investigator should approve any proposed correction to the chronology and retain the earlier version.

This provides an audit trail: a record of what changed, why it changed and which evidence justified the revision.

What can missing footage actually tell investigators?

A gap in a UFO recording is not necessarily a problem that can be solved. Sometimes it establishes a firm limit on what can be concluded.

Consider a case involving two short clips recorded from the same garden.

The first shows a bright object moving slowly towards a tree. The second, recorded approximately 90 seconds later, shows a similar light beyond the tree.

Several interpretations are possible. The object may have continued along the same path, changed direction while hidden, remained stationary for part of the interval, or been replaced in the witness’s view by another light.

Without additional evidence, the investigator cannot confidently reconstruct the missing 90 seconds.

The appropriate response is to mark the interval as unobserved and test whether the proposed explanations remain possible under different movement assumptions.

Distinguish disappearance from loss of visibility

A particularly important distinction concerns how the sighting ended.

A light that vanishes behind a building has not necessarily disappeared from the sky. An object that fades below the camera’s detection threshold has not necessarily ceased emitting light.

Similarly, a witness saying that an object”shot away”may be describing a rapid apparent movement, an abrupt loss of visibility or an interpretation formed after the event.

The timeline should record the directly observed change before assigning a physical explanation.

A precise description might read:

“At approximately 21:17, the light was no longer visible in the recording. Its last recorded position was adjacent to the roofline. The available footage does not establish whether it continued behind the building or ceased to be visible for another reason.”

This is more informative than describing the event simply as an unexplained disappearance.

Avoid false precision in reconstructed paths

Software can draw smooth curves between observed positions, but the smoothness of a graphic does not make the underlying motion certain.

If only three positions are known, numerous paths may fit them.

Interpolation can be useful for illustrating one possible movement, provided it is clearly distinguished from the actual observations. It should not be used to manufacture evidence of steady speed, acceleration or a sudden turn.

Where uncertainty is substantial, a broad possible movement corridor may be more appropriate than a single trajectory.

How does the timeline help test ordinary explanations?

Once the event sequence is reasonably established, it becomes possible to compare the observation with other dated and located events.

The most useful comparison is often not a general resemblance between two UFO reports, but whether a candidate explanation could have occupied the reported viewing direction during the relevant time window.

For example, an aircraft hypothesis should be checked against the times and directions of the recorded movement. An astronomical explanation must be compatible with the object’s apparent position and the period during which it was observed. A satellite or re-entry hypothesis requires a plausible time and viewing path.

The timeline makes these comparisons testable.

A candidate explanation might fit the reported appearance but fail because it occurred 40 minutes later. Another might match the time but be inconsistent with the viewing direction.

Conversely, a witness’s estimated time may be sufficiently uncertain that a small discrepancy does not rule out a match.

Investigators should therefore compare candidates against the complete supported time window rather than only a convenient central timestamp.

NASA’s UAP study specifically identified the value of cross-correlating reported events with other datasets, provided that observations and their metadata are sufficiently reliable. NASA

The outcome should distinguish between a candidate that is ruled out by reliable evidence, one that remains plausible, and one that cannot be assessed because essential timing or positional information is missing.

A lack of matching information is not itself proof of unusual technology.

What mistakes most often distort a reconstructed sighting?

Several recurring problems can turn a reasonable collection of observations into an unreliable chronology.

Treating estimates as measurements. A witness’s”roughly five minutes”should not become exactly 300 seconds unless independent evidence supports that precision.

Trusting every device timestamp. Cameras and recording systems may have incorrect clocks, altered metadata or undocumented time offsets. NIST’s work on video exchange standards highlights why reliable clock information is essential when synchronising evidence. NIST

Assuming every video is continuous. Missing frames, editing, conversion and recording interruptions can affect apparent timing. These issues must be checked before interpreting sudden changes in motion. SWGDE

Combining independent witnesses too early. A single agreed narrative may conceal genuine differences between observations and make it harder to determine which details were independently remembered.

Confusing camera motion with object motion. A changing background or moving observer can create apparent speed that does not represent the object’s physical movement.

Filling gaps with AI-generated events. A plausible reconstruction is not equivalent to evidence that an event occurred.

Assuming the first or last observation marks the object’s actual arrival or departure. A witness may notice an object after it has been visible for some time, or lose sight of it while it remains present.

These errors are avoidable when the reconstruction preserves original statements, technical measurements and investigator interpretations as separate layers.

When is a UFO sighting timeline reliable enough to use?

A timeline does not need to be complete to be useful. It needs to be transparent about what is known and sufficiently precise for the question being investigated.

For instance, a sighting known only to have occurred sometime between 20:00 and 21:00 may still support a broad check of aircraft activity or astronomical conditions. It may not support confident identification of a particular aircraft that passed through the area at 20:37.

Likewise, a recording with accurate relative timing but an unknown start time may be useful for assessing apparent movement while remaining unsuitable for precise comparison with external events.

The finished case file should allow another investigator to determine:

  • Which events are directly supported by recordings or other independent evidence.
  • Which times come from witness estimates.
  • Which timestamps have been corrected, and how.
  • Where observations overlap or contradict one another.
  • Which periods remain unobserved.
  • Which conclusions depend on assumptions about movement or object identity.

The distinction between incomplete evidence and genuinely unexplained evidence is important.

GEIPAN’s published classification system separates cases that cannot be identified because information is insufficient from cases that remain unidentified after investigation. GEIPAN

A reconstructed timeline can help establish which situation applies. If essential timing information is missing, the correct finding may be that the available evidence cannot discriminate between competing explanations.

If multiple independent observations establish a consistent sequence, with well-supported times and directions, the sighting may warrant more detailed investigation even when its cause remains unknown.

The strongest reconstruction is therefore not necessarily the one with the most entries or the most detailed movement diagram. It is the one that makes every important event traceable, every correction understandable and every remaining gap explicit.

That is what turns an incomplete UFO account into a useful, evidence-led chronology within a reliable sighting case file.
